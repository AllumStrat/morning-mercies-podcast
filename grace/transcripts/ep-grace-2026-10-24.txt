Father, into your hands I commit my spirit. Those are the last words Luke records from Jesus, and they are not a cry of defeat. They are a bedtime prayer. A child's prayer, said by a man in agony, to the one he has called Father since the very beginning. And a few pages later the writer of Hebrews will ask what it looks like to live for things you cannot see. The answer, it turns out, is that it looks a great deal like this.

Good morning, Grace. This is Morning Mercies, and today is plan day nine nineteen. We have three readings. Proverbs twenty-seven, verses fifteen through twenty-seven. Luke twenty-three, verses forty-four through forty-nine. And Hebrews eleven, verses one through sixteen.

Yesterday a dying thief asked to be remembered, and Hebrews told us we may enter by a new and living way through the curtain. Today the curtain of the temple itself finally tears. And in Luke you will notice the crowds going home beating their breasts, which should remind us of what Jesus told the women of Jerusalem on the road, that they should weep for themselves and for their children. We will come back to that. But first we go to Proverbs, where Solomon has some very down to earth things to say about a leaky roof, a sharp knife, and a flock of sheep.

Proverbs twenty-seven is still part of the collection that Hezekiah's men copied out, so we are reading Solomon's sayings as they were gathered and arranged about two hundred and fifty years later. Today's section begins with a sharp comparison, and ends with a gentle farming poem. In between, there are a few lines that are better than they first sound.

Verse fifteen. A continual dripping on a rainy day and a quarrelsome wife are alike. Verse sixteen adds that to restrain her is to restrain the wind or to grasp oil in the right hand. The picture is of a roof that leaks, in a house with no way to go outside because it is pouring. John Gill says the point is that a man in that situation has, in his words, "no comfort at home." Notice what the proverb is actually describing. It is not mocking a woman. It is describing a home that has stopped being a refuge, and you cannot fix a drip by pushing on it. The same book has already said it twice, in chapter nineteen and again in chapter twenty-one, so the writer clearly saw it often. And anyone, husband or wife, can be the drip. A household where every conversation turns into a quarrel wears down the people inside it the way water wears down stone.

Then verse seventeen, which is probably the best known line of the chapter. Iron sharpens iron, and one man sharpens another. The Hebrew picture is of a blade being drawn across a blade, and the thing that makes a knife keen is friction. Matthew Poole says that in good company "his very wits are sharpened, and his spirit revived." Gill gives it a scholar's flavor, saying that "thus learned men sharpen one another's minds, and excite each other to learned studies." Both are right, but notice that the proverb does not say iron sharpens iron gently. A blade sharpened on another blade makes sparks. And that links to what we read yesterday in Hebrews, to stir one another up to love and good works. Meeting together is not a social habit. It is where we get our edge.

Verse eighteen says whoever tends a fig tree will eat its fruit, and whoever guards his master will be honored. Slow, faithful work gets rewarded in time. Verse nineteen says as in water face reflects face, so the heart of man reflects the man. When you look into still water you see your own face. When you look at what a person loves and fears and hopes for, you see their heart. Verse twenty says Sheol and Abaddon are never satisfied, and neither are the eyes of man. Death never says enough. And the eyes of a human being, left to themselves, do not either. That is the hunger that drives so much of our wanting.

Verse twenty-one gives a test. The crucible is for silver, and the furnace is for gold, and a man is tested by his praise. We usually think that suffering tests us. Solomon says that praise tests us as well. Heat shows what is pure and what is not. When people say good things about you, what rises to the top? Verse twenty-two adds that even if you crush a fool in a mortar with a pestle along with grain, his folly will not depart from him. Some people do not learn even when they are ground down.

Then the chapter changes its voice. Verse twenty-three. Know well the condition of your flocks, and give attention to your herds. Verse twenty-four gives the reason, for riches do not last forever, and a crown is not secure for all generations. Verses twenty-five to twenty-seven describe the whole cycle. The grass is gone, the new growth appears, the vegetation of the mountains is gathered, the lambs will provide your clothing, the goats the price of a field, and there will be enough goat's milk for your food and for the food of your household. It is a lovely picture of a whole household fed and clothed by attention and care.

Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, says "We ought to have some business to do in this world, and not to live in idleness." And Matthew Poole states the aim plainly. Know the state of your flocks, "that thou mayst preserve and improve what thou hast." Notice the word know. The Hebrew idiom in the verse is to know the face of your flock. A shepherd who could read the faces of his sheep would catch a limp before it became a loss. The last section of the chapter is saying that diligence is mostly attention. So here is the application, and it is a small one. What is the thing in your life that is truly yours to tend? A family, a friendship, a job, a body, a habit of prayer. Do not trust that it will go well because you once did well by it. Look at its face today.

Now to Luke. We have come to the hour. Verse forty-four says it was now about the sixth hour, which is noon, and there was darkness over the whole land until the ninth hour, which is three in the afternoon. Luke adds that the sun's light failed.

This is the first hard question, so let us deal with it. Was it an eclipse? John Gill says that if it was, it was no ordinary one. He writes, "There was an eclipse of it, which was preternatural, it being now full moon, and lasted three hours." The Cambridge Bible for Schools agrees, saying that it could not have been due to an eclipse, because the Passover moon was at the full. A solar eclipse can only happen at the new moon, and it never lasts three hours. So whatever Luke is describing, it was not a natural event on a calendar. Darkness in the Old Testament is the sign of judgment. In Exodus there was a darkness over Egypt that could be felt, and Amos spoke of a day when the sun would go down at noon. Luke does not explain it. He just lets it hang over the cross. The one who made light is dying, and the day goes dark.

Then verse forty-five. The curtain of the temple was torn in the middle. Notice that Luke tells us this before he tells us Jesus died, where Matthew and Mark put it after. Luke seems to want it to stand beside the darkness, as part of the sign. The Cambridge Bible says the obvious meaning was "the departure of the Shechinah or Presence of God from His now-deserted Temple." That is a heavy reading. It means the house in Jerusalem is being left. But Hebrews, which we read yesterday, gives the other side of the same event. The curtain is not only a sign of a house being abandoned. It is the way into the holy place opened wide. The same tearing that says the old temple is finished says to you, come in.

And now verse forty-six, which is the center of the passage. Jesus, calling out with a loud voice, said, Father, into your hands I commit my spirit. And having said this he breathed his last. Two things stand out. First, this is Psalm thirty-one, verse five, an evening prayer of trust. Jesus takes the words of that psalm and adds one word at the start, which is Father. Notice also what Luke leaves out. Luke does not record the cry, why have you forsaken me. That is in Matthew and Mark. Luke gives us the first prayer, Father forgive them, and the last, Father, into your hands. Both begin with Father. Pulpit Commentary notes the centurion hearing this, saying that "Twice in those solemn hours had the centurion heard the Crucified pray to his Father."

The second thing is the Greek verb. Commit, in Luke, is paratithemai, which means to place something beside someone for safekeeping, like a deposit in a bank. Jesus is giving his spirit to the Father as a trust. Gill is careful about what that spirit is, saying that it is "not the Holy Spirit, nor his divine nature, but his human soul." So here is a man, truly a man, handing his life to God as something that will be kept. And notice the verb is present, I commit. Jesus is not having his life taken away. He is laying it down. That is what the loud voice means. It is the voice of someone with strength left to say what he chooses to say.

Spurgeon preached on this text in June of 1889, in a sermon called Our Lord's Last Cry from the Cross. He says, "Learn the next doctrine, that dying is going home to our Father." Not into the dark, not into nothing. Home. And he gives a very practical wish for every believer. "Let us, however we may die, make this our last emotion if not our last expression, Father, receive my spirit." Spurgeon was saying that the way to meet the end is to practice the prayer before it comes.

Then Luke gives us three responses, one after another. Verse forty-seven. The centurion saw what had taken place, and he praised God, saying, certainly this man was innocent. Matthew Henry says the death of Christ was, as he puts it, "magnified by the wonders that attended it." The Roman soldier who had overseen hundreds of executions sees something he cannot classify, and the word he uses can mean either innocent or righteous. Luke has been building a case for Jesus' innocence the whole way. Pilate said it. Herod said it. The thief said it. Now the soldier says it, and he does it by glorifying God.

Verse forty-eight. All the crowds who had assembled for this spectacle, when they saw what had taken place, returned home beating their breasts. This is the answer to the women on the road. Jesus told them to weep for themselves, and now the whole crowd goes home with the grief in their own chests. They came for a show and left with a conscience. Then verse forty-nine, which is quiet. All his acquaintances and the women who had followed him from Galilee stood at a distance watching these things. The women have been with him since chapter eight. They will be there at the burial, and they will be there on Sunday morning. They are the ones who keep watch.

So here is the application. Spurgeon wanted us to pray this prayer at the end. I would like you to pray it tonight, at the beginning of the end of the day. Before sleep, say what Jesus said. Father, into your hands I commit my spirit. Then add the things you cannot hold, a person you worry about, a decision, a fear. It is a way of handing over the keys. You do not have to understand how it will turn out. A deposit is safe because of the one who holds it, not because of the one who gives it. The one who prayed it first knows exactly what it cost.

Now the letter to the Hebrews. Chapter eleven is the most famous chapter in the book, but it does not begin a new subject. Look at where chapter ten ended. The righteous one shall live by faith, and we are not of those who shrink back, but of those who have faith and preserve their souls. Chapter eleven is the writer explaining what that kind of faith looks like, by walking the readers through a gallery of people who lived it.

Verse one gives the definition. Now faith is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen. The two key words are Greek. The first is hupostasis, which can mean a foundation, a title deed, or the real substance of something. The second is elegchos, which means proof or conviction. Faith is a solid ground under your feet, and it is evidence for what you cannot see. Matthew Henry says, "It is a firm persuasion and expectation, that God will perform all he has promised to us in Christ." R.C. Sproul, in a teaching series on faith, asks the obvious question, whether that is just wishful thinking. He answers, "But that faith is an evidence because its object is God, who has a track record and who is infallible." Faith is not a leap in the dark. It is trust in someone whose record you can read.

Verse two says by it the people of old received their commendation. Verse three says by faith we understand that the universe was created by the word of God, so that what is seen was not made out of things that are visible. That is the first example, and it is one that every reader shares. None of us were there. We believe in creation by faith, and the writer says that is where faith starts.

Then come the names. Abel in verse four, who offered a more acceptable sacrifice, and through his faith, though he died, still speaks. Chrysostom, in his homily on this verse, says, "He honored God, and died because he honored Him: and has not yet attained to a resurrection." Notice the weight of that. Abel's faith got him killed, and he is still waiting. That is the pattern we will see throughout the chapter.

Enoch in verse five was taken up so that he should not see death, and the writer says that before he was taken he was commended as having pleased God. Verse six is the center of this section. Without faith it is impossible to please him, for whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him. Notice that it says draw near, the same verb as in Hebrews ten, verse twenty-two. Faith is the way you come in. Noah in verse seven was warned about things not yet seen, and he built an ark. The warning was about rain, and there had never been rain. He took God at his word.

Then the writer slows down for Abraham, and the longer passage is the heart of it. Verse eight. By faith Abraham obeyed when he was called to go out to a place that he was to receive as an inheritance, and he went out, not knowing where he was going. Verse nine says by faith he went to live in the land of promise as in a foreign land, living in tents with Isaac and Jacob. Verse ten tells why. For he was looking forward to the city that has foundations, whose designer and builder is God. Think of that. A man living in tents, looking for a city with foundations. Tents have no foundations. Sarah is next, in verse eleven, and God gave her power to conceive when she was past the age, since she considered him faithful who had promised. And verse twelve says from one man, and him as good as dead, were born descendants as many as the stars of heaven.

Now we reach the hardest verse of the section, verse thirteen. These all died in faith, not having received the things promised, but having seen them and greeted them from afar. That is a startling thing to say. They died without getting it. What is the writer claiming? He is not saying their faith failed. He is saying that faith can be completely real and still not see its object in this life. They saw the promises from a distance, and greeted them, the way you wave at a ship you cannot yet board. They confessed that they were strangers and exiles on the earth.

The writer then draws the logic out. Verse fourteen says people who speak this way make it clear that they are seeking a homeland. Verse fifteen notes that if they had been thinking of the land they had left, they would have had opportunity to return. But verse sixteen says they desire a better country, that is, a heavenly one. Therefore, God is not ashamed to be called their God, for he has prepared for them a city.

Hear that last line again. God is not ashamed. Gill comments, "This proves that he is not ashamed of the relation he stands in to them." Matthew Poole describes the country they wanted as "a country where there is perfection of life, and fulness of glory." God has put his name to these tent dwellers. He is called the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and he says it with pride. Chrysostom, preaching on this part of the chapter, draws the line to his own congregation. "Let us even now become strangers; that God may not be ashamed of us to be called our God." Think about what that asks of you. If this is not home, you can hold the house and the job and the plans a little more loosely, and still do all of it well, as Proverbs just told us to. A stranger can tend a flock faithfully. He just does not mistake it for the promised country.

Let me pull a few threads together, because I think they are real. The first is the prayer in Luke and the pride in Hebrews. Jesus says Father, receive my spirit, and Hebrews says God is not ashamed to be called the God of those who trusted him. A Father who receives, and a God who owns. They go together. Second, seen and unseen. The darkness at noon, and the faith in things not seen. At the cross, the sun was not seen. And Abraham saw a city that was not there yet. Third, the shepherd in Proverbs who learns the face of every sheep, and the Son who, at the end of his work, hands everything he has been given back into his Father's keeping. Faithful tending and faithful trusting turn out to be the same habit, practiced at different sizes.

So here is the question to carry through the day. What are you holding that you could hand over? And is there something you are tending today that you have stopped looking at closely?

Tomorrow we are at the burial. Luke twenty-three, verses fifty through fifty-six, with Joseph of Arimathea and the women preparing spices. Hebrews eleven continues, with Abraham offering Isaac and the faith of Moses. And Proverbs twenty-eight, verses one through fourteen, begins with the wicked who flee when no one pursues.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
