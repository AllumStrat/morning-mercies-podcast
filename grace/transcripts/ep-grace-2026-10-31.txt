In the beginning was the Word. Four words in Greek that open the most-read page in the world. And then, a few pages later in the day's reading, a different man says, All is vanity. A breath. A puff of steam. Two ways of beginning, and both of them are in your Bible this morning.

Welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day ten, day one, and today we have three readings. In the Gospel of John, chapter one, verses one through eighteen, the great prologue. In James, chapter one, verses one through eleven, trials and the wisdom to face them. And in Ecclesiastes chapter one, the Preacher and his famous opening line.

Notice that today three books begin at once. That is a gift, because each of them opens by telling you how to read everything that follows.

Yesterday we ended with Luke and Hebrews both finishing on a blessing, with Jesus lifting his hands, and the writer of Hebrews praying that God would equip his people. The point was that the Christian life starts with something given, and not something produced. Hold on to that. Today John says it in his own way, in verse twelve. To all who received him, he gave the right to become children of God. Given. Received. We will come back to it.

Let us begin where the Preacher begins, in Ecclesiastes.

The book opens with a title. The words of the Preacher, the son of David, king in Jerusalem. The Hebrew word is Qoheleth, and it comes from a word for gathering. Some render it Preacher, some Teacher, some Assembler. He is someone who gathers a crowd, and someone who gathers up what he has learned. Christian tradition from early on has heard Solomon behind the voice, the son of David who had wealth, wisdom and time to test everything. And the book itself will go on to describe exactly that kind of experiment.

The poem is built in two halves. Verses two and three give the motto and the question. Verses four through eleven prove it from nature and history. Then verses twelve through eighteen turn personal, with the king describing his own search for wisdom.

Start with the motto in verse two. Vanity of vanities, says the Preacher, vanity of vanities! All is vanity. The word translated vanity is hebel. The Pulpit Commentary explains it plainly. Quote. Vanity is hebel, which means breath, and is used metaphorically of anything transitory, frail, unsatisfying. End quote. It is also the very name Abel, the second son of Adam, whose life was a breath, gone almost before it began. So hebel is not a heavy word like emptiness or meaninglessness. It is light. Something you can see for a moment on a cold morning and then it is gone. The old Latin scholar Jerome, the Cambridge Bible notes, preferred the rendering vapor of vapors. And vanity of vanities is the Hebrew way of saying the most, the way we say song of songs.

Then the question in verse three. What does man gain by all the toil at which he toils under the sun? The Cambridge Bible catches the flavor of the Hebrew word for gain. It means, quote, the surplus, if any, of the balance-sheet of life. End quote. That is a businessman's word. After all the labor is added up, what is left over on the page?

And notice the phrase that will return nearly thirty times in this book. Under the sun. It does not mean the whole universe. It means life viewed from the ground, on this side of the grave, as far as the eye and the calendar can take you. Keep that phrase in your pocket. It is the key to the whole book.

Now the proof. Verses four through eleven are a little poem about circles. A generation goes and a generation comes, but the earth remains. The sun rises, hastens to where it rose, and rises again. The wind goes south, turns north, goes around and around. The streams run to the sea, and the sea is never full. Nothing here is broken. This is creation doing exactly what God made it to do. And the Preacher hears in it a kind of weariness, because everything moves, and nothing arrives.

Then verse eight. All things are full of weariness, a man cannot utter it, the eye is not satisfied with seeing, nor the ear filled with hearing. Have you ever scrolled for an hour, and put the phone down emptier than you picked it up? That is verse eight. The eye is never full.

Verse nine says what has been is what will be, and there is nothing new under the sun. Matthew Henry, the Presbyterian minister whose commentary on the whole Bible has served the church for three centuries, wrote on these verses. Quote. This should take us from expecting happiness in the creature, and quicken us to seek eternal blessings. End quote. By creature he means anything made. The cycles are meant to teach you to stop expecting a created thing to do what only the Creator can.

Verse eleven finishes the thought with a sting. No one remembers the former things, and no one will remember the latest. Your name, in other words, will go the way of the kings whose names we cannot recall.

Then the king speaks of himself. Verse thirteen. I applied my heart to seek and to search out by wisdom all that is done under heaven. Two verbs for searching, one that digs down and one that surveys wide. And his verdict is that God has given this unhappy business to the children of man to be busy with. Notice, God has given it. The Preacher is not an atheist. He believes in a God who has set a limit around human understanding. In verse fifteen, what is crooked cannot be made straight. Something in the world is bent, and no amount of cleverness will straighten it.

Verse eighteen is the line that every student has underlined. In much wisdom is much vexation, and he who increases knowledge increases sorrow. More knowledge, more grief. The more you see, the more you see wrong.

So here is the hardest question in the chapter. Is the Preacher telling us that life is hopeless? Matthew Henry puts the hinge exactly where it belongs. Quote. If this world, in its present state, were all, it would not be worth living for. End quote. If this world were all. Everything in chapter one is true under the sun. The book does not end here. By its last chapter the Preacher will tell you to fear God and keep his commandments, and we will walk toward that slowly over the coming days. But do not rush past this. A faith that cannot admit the breath-like quality of life has not yet looked at life. Grace, you might say, begins by being honest. The Preacher gives you permission to say what you already know to be true, that you cannot make the circle stop, and you cannot make the crooked straight. He clears the ground so that something given can stand there.

The application is small and honest. Tonight, when you feel the weariness of verse eight, do not call it a failure of faith. Call it what the Preacher called it, and then bring it to the One who made the sun.

Now to the Gospel, which is the heart of today.

John opens his Gospel without a genealogy and without a birth story. No Bethlehem, no shepherds. He starts before time. In the beginning was the Word. Those first three words are the first three words of Genesis in the Greek Old Testament, so any Jewish reader would hear it at once. John is writing a new Genesis. And the Greek word is logos, which means word, but also reason, message, the thing that expresses a mind. Everyone from a Greek philosopher to a Jewish rabbi had something to say about the logos, and John deliberately steps into the middle of that conversation.

The apostle John wrote near the end of the first century, according to the church's oldest tradition from Ephesus. He had seen it all. And what is so striking is how he puts it. Chrysostom, the golden-mouthed preacher of fourth-century Antioch, preached on this Gospel and began his second homily with the writer himself. He says John was a poor fisherman from Galilee, whose father was a fisherman, a man who had no education to speak of. Then he says, quote, But fear not; we shall hear nought of these; but we shall hear of things in heaven, and what no one ever learned before this man. End quote. Nought means nothing. A man who smelled of nets and lake water writes the most exalted sentence in the Bible. Chrysostom's point is that the words came from beyond him.

Let me walk it. Verses one and two make three claims in a row. The Word was in the beginning, the Word was with God, the Word was God. Each clause pushes higher. He is eternal, he is distinct, and he is divine. Not a created being, not a second god, not merely a power. Augustine, preaching on this very verse in North Africa, asked the question any careful listener would ask. Quote. Did not those words which we uttered sound and pass away? Did God's Word, then, sound and come to an end? End quote. Augustine's answer is no. When we speak, a word is a noise that fades. But the Word who was with God is the thought of God, speaking God, eternal as God. This is the heart of what the church later confessed in the Nicene Creed, that the Son is eternally begotten of the Father.

Verse three says that all things were made through him. Nothing that was made was made without him. Then verse four, in him was life, and the life was the light of men. Verse five is one of the great sentences in all of Scripture. The light shines in the darkness, and the darkness has not overcome it. Shines is in the present tense. It is still shining. And the word overcome carries a double sense in Greek, to seize, and to understand. The darkness neither grasped the light nor put it out.

Then the prologue is interrupted by a witness. A man sent from God, whose name was John, not the apostle but the Baptist. He is not the light, he is the witness to it. Both in verses six through eight, and again in verse fifteen, he appears like a signpost. His message is brief. He was before me.

Now the saddest verses in the passage. Verse ten. He was in the world, and the world was made through him, yet the world did not know him. Verse eleven. He came to his own, and his own people did not receive him. Think of what that says. The Maker walks into his own workshop, and no one knows who he is.

But verse twelve turns on the little word but. To all who did receive him, who believed in his name, he gave the right to become children of God. And verse thirteen says how. Not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God. You cannot inherit it, you cannot decide it into being, and no one can arrange it for you. That is what we said at the start. It is given.

Then comes verse fourteen, the summit. And the Word became flesh and dwelt among us. Two words need a closer look. Flesh is not a polite word. John could have said body, or man. He chose flesh, the frail, hungry, mortal stuff. The Cambridge Bible says this is the gulf between John and the Jewish philosopher Philo, who wrote much about the Word. Philo would have agreed with everything up to this point, it says, but from this he would have shrunk.

The second word is dwelt. In Greek it is eskenosen. It means pitched his tent, tabernacled. Think back to the wilderness, when God's glory filled the tabernacle and traveled with Israel. John is saying the glory has moved into a human life. John Gill, the Baptist pastor of London, drew out the picture. Quote. The human nature of Christ was of God's pitching, and not man's; and though it looked mean without, the fulness of the Godhead dwelt in it. End quote. Mean there means plain, ordinary. To the people of Nazareth he looked like a carpenter's son.

And we have seen his glory, John says, glory as of the only Son from the Father. The Greek is monogenes, one of a kind, the only one. Full of grace and truth. Spurgeon preached on this verse, and his outline survives. He says of Jesus, quote, There is grace in other men; but they have it as water flowing through a pipe: He as water in its fountain. End quote. Other people can pass grace along. Jesus is the source.

Verse sixteen goes on. From his fullness we have all received, grace upon grace. Alexander MacLaren, the great Baptist preacher of Manchester, noticed the strange little word for. Grace for grace. Quote. As one supply of grace is given and used, it is, as it were, given back to the Bestower, who substitutes for it a fresh and unused vessel, filled with new grace. End quote. Picture it. You come in the morning with an empty jar, and he does not refill the old one, he hands you a new one, full.

Verse seventeen draws a contrast that is easy to misread. The law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ. John is not setting law against grace as enemies. Moses gave, and Christ brought. The law was a gift too. But what the law pointed to has now arrived in person.

And the last verse. No one has ever seen God. The only God, who is at the Father's side, has made him known. The Greek verb for made known is exegesato, from which we get exegesis. Jesus is the explanation of God. The Cambridge Bible says the purpose of the incarnation is that, quote, the truth cannot be fully known, while God is not fully revealed. End quote. If you want to know what God is like, you do not need to guess. You look at him.

So here is the question the passage presses on you. John says some received him and some did not. What does it mean, in the ordinary hours of your Saturday, to receive the Word? It is simply to take what is given. Not to polish yourself first, not to wait until you feel ready. You come empty, and you take the new jar. And notice that this is the same posture James asks of you in his letter, a hand held out for wisdom, and the same one the Preacher leaves you with when everything under the sun has run through your fingers. Three books, three mornings of the same invitation. Take what is given.

Now to James.

James is likely the earliest of the New Testament letters, written in the forties or fifties of the first century. The author is James, the brother of the Lord, who became the leader of the church in Jerusalem. Notice how he introduces himself. Not as brother of Jesus. A servant of God and of the Lord Jesus Christ. He is addressing the twelve tribes in the Dispersion, Jewish Christians scattered after the persecution that followed Stephen. These are people who lost their homes and their security.

The letter reads like a collection of short sermons, and it is full of wisdom. The Cambridge Bible says of James, quote, It is as though he had largely fashioned his thoughts of the spiritual life on the teaching of the Book of Proverbs and Ecclesiastes. End quote. That is a real connection to today, and we will return to it.

Verse two begins with a command that sounds absurd. Count it all joy, my brothers, when you meet trials of various kinds. Not feel joy. Count it. The verb is an accounting word, like the Preacher's balance sheet. Reckon it. And the reason is in verse three. You know that the testing of your faith produces steadfastness. The word is hypomone, a staying under. Not a quick escape but a patient endurance. Verse four. Let steadfastness have its full effect, that you may be perfect and complete, lacking in nothing. Perfect here means whole, mature, not sinless.

Now verse five, and watch the connection. Right after lacking in nothing, he says, if any of you lacks wisdom. In the middle of trials you do not know what to do. So ask God, who gives generously to all without reproach. Generously carries the idea of singleness, of no strings. Without reproach means he does not scold you for asking again. Matthew Henry says it better than most. Quote. We should not pray so much for the removal of affliction, as for wisdom to make a right use of it. End quote. That is a hard sentence, and a freeing one. Pray for the trial to end, certainly. But pray first to know what to do inside it.

Verse six sets the condition. Ask in faith, with no doubting. The doubter is like a wave of the sea driven and tossed by the wind. Vincent's Word Studies, quoted in the Pulpit Commentary, adds that the Greek word does not simply mean unbelief. It means someone who hesitates, balancing between faith and unbelief. John Gill describes such a person as, quote, troubled, restless, unquiet, and impatient; and he is fickle, inconstant, unstable, and unsettled. End quote. And verse eight gives the name. Double-minded. In Greek, dipsychos, two-souled. A man with one foot on each of two boats.

Notice what James is not saying. He is not saying that a single doubt cancels the prayer. He is describing someone who asks God for wisdom while planning to take advice from every other source and keep his options open. Asking in faith means you come meaning to do what he says.

Then, abruptly, verses nine through eleven turn to money. Let the lowly brother boast in his exaltation, and the rich in his humiliation, because like a flower of the grass he will pass away. The sun rises with scorching heat and withers the grass. Doesn't that sound like Ecclesiastes? The sun rising, and the passing away, and the man whose pursuits fade. James reaches for the same picture as the Preacher. The poor believer is told to boast in his high standing with God. The rich believer is told to boast in being brought low, because everything he owns is grass in the sun. For both, the ground is the same.

That is where the readings meet, and the connection is real. The Preacher says all is breath, and James says the rich man is a flower in the heat. One of them says it with a puzzled sigh, and the other says it with a command to rejoice, because James knows what the Preacher was still waiting to hear. Trials strip away the false securities, and they leave you needing wisdom that you cannot produce.

So here is the question to carry through your day. Where is your confidence resting that the sun can wither? And what would it look like to ask God for wisdom there, with a hand open and empty, expecting him to give?
