It is Friday afternoon, and the most dangerous man in Jerusalem to be seen with is dead. His friends have scattered. His closest followers are hiding behind locked doors. And a member of the very council that condemned him walks through Pilate's gates and asks for the body. Nobody asked him to. Nobody would have blamed him for staying home. Why now, and why him?

Good morning, Grace. This is Morning Mercies, and today is plan day nine twenty. We have three readings. Proverbs twenty-eight, verses one through fourteen. Luke twenty-three, verses fifty through fifty-six. And Hebrews eleven, verses seventeen through thirty-one.

Yesterday ended with the women standing at a distance, watching. Today we find out why Luke told us that. They were not merely watching a death. They were taking note of where everything happened, because they meant to come back. And in Hebrews we left Abraham living in tents, looking for a city with foundations. Today we watch him walk up a mountain with the son of the promise. But we start in Proverbs, where the chapter opens with a man running from nobody.

Proverbs twenty-eight is still in the collection that Hezekiah's men copied out. This chapter has a theme that runs under almost every verse, and it is the question of what a person is like on the inside when no one is looking. Verse one says the wicked flee when no one pursues, but the righteous are bold as a lion.

Think about that picture. Someone is running, and when you look behind him, the road is empty. John Gill says that such people "are chased with the sound of a shaken leaf, and fancy men are at their heels to destroy them." A rustling leaf is enough. Matthew Henry puts it in four words: "Sin makes men cowards." The Pulpit Commentary names the other side of it. The righteous, it says, "are undismayed in the presence of danger, because their conscience is at rest." Notice that boldness here does not come from being strong. It comes from having nothing to hide. Hold on to that, because we are about to meet a man in Luke whose courage appears out of nowhere, and I think this verse explains it.

The next several verses move from the heart to the nation. Verse two says that when a land transgresses, it has many rulers, but with a man of understanding and knowledge, its stability will long continue. Verse three has a startling picture of a poor man who oppresses the poor, and Solomon says he is like a driving rain that leaves no food. The one person who should know what poverty feels like becomes a storm that wipes out the crop. Verse four says that those who forsake the law praise the wicked, and those who keep the law strive against them. In other words, what you do with God's word decides who you admire.

Verse six is one of the great better-than sayings. Better a poor man who walks in his integrity than a rich man who is crooked in his ways. Verse eight warns that whoever increases his wealth by interest and profit gathers it for him who is generous to the poor. The wealth does not stay where it was stolen.

Then comes a verse that should stop us. Verse nine. If one turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination. This is a hard saying, and we should not soften it. What is the proverb claiming? It is not saying that God refuses the cry of a sinner who wants help. The rest of the chapter says the opposite. It is describing someone who has decided in advance that he will not obey, and who still wants God's ear for his own purposes. Matthew Henry reads it this way. "The sinner at whose prayers God is angry, is one who obstinately refuses to obey God's commands." Matthew Poole says that God "will abhor and reject his person, and all his services." The key word is obstinate. A prayer is not a vending machine, and you cannot ask God for his help while telling him to stay out of the way.

Which brings us to the center of the section, verse thirteen. Whoever conceals his transgressions will not prosper, but he who confesses and forsakes them will obtain mercy. Matthew Henry says, "He who covers his sins, shall not have any true peace." And on the other side, "He who humbly confesses his sins, with true repentance and faith, shall find mercy from God." Notice there are two verbs on the good side, and they belong together. Confess, and forsake. Matthew Poole sees the danger of splitting them, and says, "mere confession without forsaking of sin is not sufficient for salvation." The Pulpit Commentary agrees that confession alone, in its words, "without amendment," does not win pardon and mercy. Confession that does not turn around is just a moment of honesty in a long habit of hiding. The proverb wants a life that actually changes direction.

Verse fourteen closes this section. Blessed is the one who fears the Lord always, but whoever hardens his heart will fall into calamity. Here is the practical question for you. Is there something you are covering? Not a dramatic secret, necessarily. Maybe a small resentment, an unpaid apology, a habit you tell no one about. The proverb promises two things. Hiding costs you peace. Confessing and leaving it behind brings mercy. So today, say it to God plainly, and then say it to the person it belongs to, if there is one. The man who lived this out is in our next reading.

Now to Luke. We have been walking through the passion for a week, and the cross is behind us. Verse fifty introduces someone new. A man named Joseph, from the Judean town of Arimathea. Luke packs a great deal into a few phrases. He is a member of the council, so he belongs to the same body that handed Jesus over to Pilate. He is a good and righteous man. And he had not consented to their decision and action. He was, Luke says, waiting for the kingdom of God.

That last detail matters. Luke has used the same language earlier, about Simeon and Anna in the temple, who were also waiting. Joseph belongs to that quiet remnant in Israel that was looking for God to act. So he is not a convert of Good Friday. But he was an invisible disciple, and we know from John's Gospel that he had kept it a secret out of fear of the Jews. The man who feared the council is now walking into Pilate's headquarters.

Here is the first question the passage raises, and it is a good one. Why did he wait until now? Why not speak up in the council, or during the trial? John Chrysostom, preaching on this in Homily eighty-eight on Matthew, notices the change. Joseph "had become very bold after the death of Christ." Think about how strange that is. You might expect the death of Jesus to make a timid man more timid. The movement has been crushed, and the leader is publicly shamed. Instead, something in Joseph breaks open. Chrysostom says that by laying the body in his own new tomb, "he shows his love, and his courage."

Spurgeon preached on this in 1888, in a sermon called A Royal Funeral, and he said something that fits the Proverbs we just read. "God can make a coward bold as a lion in the day when He needs him!" Proverbs said the righteous are bold as a lion. Spurgeon is saying that boldness can arrive late, and it can arrive suddenly. Joseph did not go to Pilate because he had finally become brave. He went because there was a body that needed a grave, and no one else would do it. He did the next thing in front of him. Notice who stepped forward. The disciples who had followed Jesus in public had scattered, and the man who came forward was the one who had kept his faith quiet.

Verse fifty-two. He went to Pilate and asked for the body of Jesus. Mark adds that Pilate was surprised Jesus was already dead, and checked with the centurion before granting it. That detail is important, because it means the Roman governor officially confirmed the death. Then in verse fifty-three, Joseph took it down, wrapped it in a linen shroud, and laid him in a tomb cut in stone, where no one had ever yet been laid. The Cambridge Bible says the linen was "in a sindon, or piece of fine white linen." That is the Greek word sindon, and it describes a good cloth, the kind you would buy for someone you honor.

Why does it matter that the tomb was new? Gill gives one reason: "so that it could not be said it was another body, and not that of Christ's." No bones to confuse things, no other body to mistake for his. Spurgeon enjoyed the point too. "Ah, well, Joseph's is a new tomb," he says, "there are no bones there, for nobody has ever been buried there before!" The Pulpit Commentary adds a gentler reason, saying these details show "that the Lord's sacred body was not brought into contact with corruption." Whether we choose the practical reason or the reverent one, the point is that Luke wants us to have no doubt about whose body went in, and which tomb it was.

Now notice the hurry. Verse fifty-four. It was the day of Preparation, and the Sabbath was beginning. The law said that a body hanging on a tree should not remain all night, and the Sabbath began at sundown. Matthew Henry says it simply: "Christ was buried in haste, because the sabbath drew on." Everything is done quickly, and it is done incompletely. They did not have time to anoint the body. That is why the women go home in verse fifty-six to prepare spices and ointments, and then, Luke says, they rested on the Sabbath according to the commandment.

I love that last line. Those women had just lost the one they loved most. Their plans were in ruins. And what they did was keep the Sabbath. They followed the law right up to the edge of the tomb. Grief did not give them permission to stop obeying. And they were rewarded for it, because the first people at the empty tomb were women who had waited.

Calvin, in his Harmony of the Gospels, takes a larger view of the whole scene. On Matthew's account of the same burial, he says, "Let us know then that, when the Son of God was buried by the hand of Joseph, it was the work of God." That is a big claim. It means Joseph's act of courage was also the Father's act of care. God arranged for his Son's body to be honored instead of thrown into a common pit, as often happened to the crucified. And Calvin goes on to say that God intended the new tomb "to point out by the sepulcher itself his newness of life." The grave was prepared for an exit.

So here is the application. Joseph did not wait until he felt brave. He saw what was needed and did the one thing that he could. You may be waiting for a certain boldness before you speak up for Christ, or make a hard call, or show kindness to someone whom others have abandoned. Joseph's example says to stop waiting for the feeling. Do the thing that is in front of you, today, and let God supply the courage as you go.

Now to Hebrews, where we pick up with Abraham. Last time, the writer gave us tents, a city with foundations, and a promise greeted from far away. Now verse seventeen turns to what must be the hardest test of Abraham's life. By faith Abraham, when he was tested, offered up Isaac. Matthew Henry calls it "the greatest trial and act of faith upon record."

Genesis twenty-two tells the story, and Hebrews gives us the inside of Abraham's mind. Verse eighteen sets up the problem. God had said that through Isaac your offspring shall be named. So the command to sacrifice Isaac contradicted the promise. Chrysostom saw this plainly in his homily on this passage. "Do you see how what was done was opposed to the promise?" he asks. God said that the line would run through Isaac, and then God said to kill Isaac. How do you obey both?

Verse nineteen is the answer. Abraham considered that God was able even to raise him from the dead. That is a remarkable step of reasoning, because nobody in Abraham's world had ever been raised from the dead. There was no precedent. But Abraham knew two things. God had promised, and God does not lie. So if the promise had to survive the death of the son, then God would have to bring him back. Chrysostom puts it this way: "he did not measure the power of God by human reasonings, but committed all to faith." Matthew Poole says that "God's almighty power to raise from the dead answered all the difficulties in the trial." A Ligonier devotional on this text says plainly that Abraham reasoned that "He could raise Isaac from the dead if need be to keep His promise."

And the verse ends with a line worth pausing on, that Abraham received him back, as a figure. The Greek word is parabole, a picture or a parable. Isaac came off the altar alive, and the writer says that was a kind of resurrection in advance. We should hold that lightly, but it is in the text. Faith, in Hebrews, is not closing your eyes. It is a conclusion about God's character, reached under pressure, and acted on.

Then the chapter speeds up. Verses twenty through twenty-two cover three deathbeds. Isaac blesses Jacob and Esau. Jacob, dying, blesses each of Joseph's sons and worships, leaning on the top of his staff. Joseph, near the end, speaks of the exodus of Israel and gives directions about his bones. Look at what these three have in common. Each is dying, and each is looking forward. Jacob is leaning on a staff, but his mind is on the promise. Joseph is lying in Egypt as its second ruler, and he asks to be carried out. His faith is that Egypt is not the end of the story, so he insists on being buried in the land that is. A man who would be laid in a borrowed tomb in Jerusalem could say the same, and we will come back to that in the close.

Verses twenty-three through twenty-nine are about Moses. First, his parents, who hid him three months because they saw that the child was beautiful, and they were not afraid of the king's edict. Then Moses himself. Verse twenty-four says that when he was grown, he refused to be called the son of Pharaoh's daughter. Verse twenty-five says he chose rather to be mistreated with the people of God than to enjoy the fleeting pleasures of sin. And verse twenty-six is the one that raises a question. He considered the reproach of Christ greater wealth than the treasures of Egypt, for he was looking to the reward.

The reproach of Christ. What can that mean about a man who lived well over a thousand years before Jesus was born? Matthew Poole says Moses chose to suffer "out of faith in and love to Christ." Gill takes it as meaning that Christ's people are one with him, and notes that Moses "counted reproach itself riches." The Cambridge Bible, noting a connection to Psalm eighty-nine, says it describes the reproach that the Messiah bears. Different explanations, but they agree on the main thing. When Moses chose with the people of God, he was already choosing the side of the one the whole Bible points toward. And Matthew Henry gives a plain summary of it, that "God's people are, and always have been, a reproached people."

Notice the reasoning in verse twenty-six, because it is the same pattern as Abraham's. Moses considered. He did sums. He weighed the treasures of Egypt against the reproach of belonging to God, and the reproach won, because he was looking to the reward. Faith in Hebrews is never irrational. It is looking at the full picture, including what cannot be seen.

Verse twenty-seven says that by faith he left Egypt, not being afraid of the anger of the king, for he endured as seeing him who is invisible. There it is again, seeing the invisible. The whole chapter hangs on that. Verse twenty-eight, by faith he kept the Passover and sprinkled the blood, so that the destroyer would not touch the firstborn. Verse twenty-nine, by faith the people crossed the Red Sea as on dry land, while the Egyptians who attempted it were drowned.

Then the writer picks two last examples from the next generation, and they are surprising. Verse thirty, by faith the walls of Jericho fell down after they had been encircled for seven days. And verse thirty-one, by faith Rahab the prostitute did not perish with those who were disobedient, because she had given a friendly welcome to the spies. After Abraham and Moses, the list includes a foreign woman with a bad reputation. The chapter does not hide that. Faith is not a matter of pedigree or past. Rahab had heard what God had done at the sea, and she concluded that this God was the true one, and she put herself on his side. That is worth sitting with for a moment. The chapter will name patriarchs, a lawgiver, and a prostitute in the same breath, and it does not apologize for any of them. What they share is not a record of success. It is that each of them, at the moment it cost something, took God at his word and acted on it. If your own past makes you feel disqualified from this list, Rahab is the answer to that feeling.

Now let me pull a few threads together, because I think they are real.

Joseph of Arimathea and Abraham both did something that looked foolish before the outcome was known. Abraham took the knife up the mountain, reasoning that God could raise the dead. Joseph put a body in a tomb, and in doing so, he put a stone over a hope. And yet in both cases, God was already arranging the answer. Calvin said the burial was the work of God. Hebrews says Abraham received his son back as a picture of resurrection. A grave, in this Bible, is never the last word.

There is also the matter of bodies and burial. The patriarch Joseph gave directions about his bones, because his faith looked past Egypt to the land of promise. Centuries later another Joseph gave his own tomb, and his faith, too, said that death is not where things end. Neither man could see the other side of the grave, and both acted as though it was there.

And Proverbs ties it together. The righteous are bold as a lion, and the one who confesses and forsakes finds mercy. The courage in Luke and in Hebrews comes from a conscience at rest, from people who have stopped running and have made peace with God.

So here is the question to carry through the day. Where is God asking you to do the next thing, when you do not yet feel the courage for it? And is there something you are still covering that you could bring into the light?

Tomorrow is the morning of the first day of the week. Luke twenty-four, verses one through twelve, where the women come with their spices. Hebrews eleven, verses thirty-two through forty, where the list of the faithful runs on to those who were mistreated and did not receive what was promised. And Proverbs twenty-eight, verses fifteen through twenty-eight.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
