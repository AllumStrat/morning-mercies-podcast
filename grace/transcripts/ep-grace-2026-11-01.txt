I am not. Three times in one conversation, a man says it. I am not the Christ. I am not Elijah. I am not the Prophet. And a few pages later, a king says the opposite. Whatever my eyes desired, I did not keep from them. One man spends his whole life saying what he is not, and he is at peace. The other has everything, and he is chasing the wind.

Welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day ten, day two, and today we have three readings. In the Gospel of John, chapter one, verses nineteen through twenty-eight, the Baptist is cross-examined. In James, chapter one, verses twelve through eighteen, the crown of life and the Father of lights. And in Ecclesiastes, chapter two, verses one through sixteen, the king tests everything he can buy.

Yesterday, Grace, three books began, and we ended on one idea: take what is given. John said it in the prologue, to all who received him he gave the right to become children of God. James told us to ask God for wisdom, because he gives to all generously. And the Preacher showed us how tired the hand gets that tries to make its own satisfaction. Today that idea gets tested hard. We watch a man who gets everything he reaches for, and a man who reaches for nothing, and see which one is rich.

Let us begin with the king.

Ecclesiastes two is the Preacher's experiment. In chapter one he searched by wisdom, and he found it was a weary business. Now he changes method. Verse one. I said in my heart, come now, I will test you with pleasure; enjoy yourself. The word heart matters. In Hebrew thought the heart is not only the feelings. It is the center of the whole person, the mind that plans. So this is a deliberate experiment. He talks to himself the way a scientist sets up a trial. And the result comes in the same verse. But behold, this also was vanity.

Remember that word, hebel. A breath, a puff of steam. It returns in this chapter again and again, and it is the verdict on every experiment.

Verse two. I said of laughter, it is mad, and of pleasure, what use is it? The Cambridge Bible, an old English commentary for students, says of that laughter that it, quote, left nothing but the cold grey ashes of a cynical satiety. End quote. Satiety means being stuffed full and still not satisfied. Think of the feeling after the third episode in a row of something you did not even enjoy. Cold grey ashes. That is the image.

Verse three is the interesting one. I searched with my heart how to cheer my body with wine, my heart still guiding me with wisdom, and how to lay hold on folly. This man is not a drunk. He is a careful investigator, holding the reins of wisdom while he samples folly. He wants to see whether there is anything good for people to do under heaven during the few days of their lives. He is trying to keep his head while he indulges his appetites. And even so, nothing.

So he moves from pleasures to projects. Verses four through eight read like the inventory of a very rich man. I made great works. I built houses and planted vineyards. I made gardens and parks, and planted all kinds of fruit trees in them. I made pools of water to irrigate a forest of growing trees. Then people, servants, and herds and flocks, more than any who had been before him in Jerusalem. Silver and gold, the treasure of kings and provinces. Singers, and the delights of the sons of men. Read the list with the book of First Kings open beside it, and you recognize Solomon. The Preacher is not exaggerating. It is the portrait of the richest and wisest king Israel ever had.

Verse nine. So I became great and surpassed all who were before me in Jerusalem. Also my wisdom remained with me. Then verse ten, which is the most honest verse in the passage. And whatever my eyes desired I did not keep from them. I kept my heart from no pleasure, for my heart found pleasure in all my toil, and this was my reward for all my toil. He admits it. The pleasure was real. It was not an illusion. He enjoyed the work of his own hands, and the Hebrew word for reward means a portion, an allotment, what you get as your share.

But then verse eleven. Then I considered all that my hands had done and the toil I had expended in doing it, and behold, all was vanity and a striving after wind, and there was nothing to be gained under the sun. Striving after wind. The picture is a man running after the breeze with a net. He runs hard. He may even catch a hint of it on his face. He never holds it.

Now the second half, verses twelve through sixteen, turns to wisdom itself. He compares wisdom and folly. And here the Preacher does not flatter folly. Verse thirteen. Then I saw that there is more gain in wisdom than in folly, as there is more gain in light than in darkness. Verse fourteen. The wise person has his eyes in his head, but the fool walks in darkness. Wisdom really is better. The Preacher believes that, and he says so. But then comes the sting. And yet I perceived that the same event happens to all of them.

Matthew Poole, the Puritan commentator, glosses that line plainly. Quote. One event happeneth to them all; both are subject to the same calamities, and to death itself. End quote. And Matthew Henry takes it one step further, in a sentence that is a good deal sharper than I expected from his short notes. Quote. The most learned of men, who dies a stranger to Christ Jesus, will perish equally with the most ignorant. End quote. Notice what Henry has done. He has quietly added a clause. The Preacher says the wise and the fool both die. Henry says the wise man who does not know Christ has nothing extra to show at the last. That is a pastor's reading, and it points the way the book will eventually go.

Verse sixteen finishes the point. For of the wise as of the fool there is no enduring remembrance, seeing that in the days to come all will have been long forgotten.

So here is the hardest question in this reading. Is the Preacher saying that pleasure is the problem? Is Ecclesiastes a sermon against enjoying life? It is not. Look again at verse ten. The enjoyment was real, and he calls it his portion. We will see in tomorrow's reading that the Preacher will say, in verse twenty-four, that there is nothing better than to eat and drink and find enjoyment in toil, and that this too is from the hand of God. So pleasure is not the enemy. The failure is in asking pleasure to be the point. Treat pleasure as the project, and it ends in ashes. Receive pleasure as a gift, and it becomes a kind of worship.

The application is small and practical. This week, take one good thing you enjoy, a meal, a walk, a song, and before you enjoy it, say thank you to the one who gave it. That one sentence is the difference between hoarding the breeze and receiving it.

Now to the Gospel.

John has told us in the prologue that a man named John was sent from God as a witness. Now in verse nineteen we meet him in action. And this is the testimony of John, when the Jews sent priests and Levites from Jerusalem to ask him, who are you?

Set the scene. The Baptist has been at the Jordan for some time, preaching repentance, and crowds have been going out to him. The religious leaders in Jerusalem, who guarded the temple and the law, cannot ignore this. So they send an official delegation, priests and Levites, and verse twenty-four adds that those who had been sent were from the Pharisees. These are the investigators. They want to know who he is claiming to be.

The place is named in verse twenty-eight. These things took place in Bethany across the Jordan, where John was baptizing. Some of your Bibles say Bethabara there. That is a real textual wrinkle. The Cambridge Bible puts it this way. Quote. The true reading is Bethany, which was changed to Bethabara owing to the powerful influence of Origen. End quote. Origen, the early Christian scholar, could not find a Bethany near the Jordan and so adopted Bethabara, which John Gill tells us means the house of passage, or a ford. The best old manuscripts read Bethany, and it was probably a small village east of the river, not the Bethany near Jerusalem where Lazarus lived. The location is lost to us. What matters is the setting: wilderness side of the Jordan, the very ground Israel had crossed to enter the land.

Now the questions. Who are you? Verse twenty. He confessed, and did not deny, but confessed, I am not the Christ. Notice the repetition. He confessed, and did not deny, but confessed. John is piling up the verbs, because the denial is itself a confession. He says no with his whole heart.

Chrysostom, preaching on this Gospel in the fourth century, was suspicious of the delegation. He thought they came to flatter John into claiming too much, thinking, he says, quote, by their flattery to induce him to confess that he was the Christ. End quote. A little flattery from the right people, and a preacher can come to believe it. John does the opposite.

Verse twenty-one. What then? Are you Elijah? He said, I am not. Are you the Prophet? And he answered, No. Elijah is from Malachi, chapter four, where God promises to send Elijah the prophet before the great and terrible day of the Lord. And the Prophet is from Deuteronomy eighteen, where Moses says the Lord will raise up for you a prophet like me. Hold the Greek for a moment. The question is literally the prophet, with the definite article. They mean the one Moses promised. Both are real hopes from the Old Testament, and the crowd was waiting for all of them.

This is the hardest question in the passage, and it is a genuine puzzle. John says, I am not Elijah. But in Matthew eleven, Jesus says of John, if you are willing to accept it, he is Elijah who is to come. How can both be true? Matthew Henry, in his concise notes on this verse, answers it cleanly. Quote. Came in the spirit and power of Elias, but he was not the person of Elias. End quote. Elias is simply the Greek form of Elijah. The Jews expected the prophet himself to come back from heaven in person. John denies that. Gabriel's words in Luke one say he would go before the Lord in the spirit and power of Elijah. So John is Elijah in function and not in identity. And John, humble to the bone, will not claim even the job title the angel gave him, because they are asking about identity, and he will not let them put him in a category that might get in the way of the real person.

So they press him. Verse twenty-two. Who are you? We need to give an answer to those who sent us. What do you say about yourself? Notice that. He has been named by Moses, by Malachi, by Isaiah, by everyone but himself. Finally he speaks. Verse twenty-three. I am the voice of one crying out in the wilderness, make straight the way of the Lord, as the prophet Isaiah said.

The Greek word for voice is phone. A sound. Not a name. Not a title. The Cambridge Bible notes that the phrase can be read, I am a voice, and then says of him, quote, John was a Voice making known the Word, meaningless without the Word. End quote. A voice with nothing to say is only noise. John's whole identity is that he has a message and the message is another person. The Pulpit Commentary says he, quote, lost himself in his office and in his message. End quote.

Then comes the question about baptism in verse twenty-five. Why then are you baptizing, if you are neither the Christ, nor Elijah, nor the Prophet? That is a sharp question. Baptism was a rite for outsiders joining Israel, for Gentile converts. John is baptizing Jews, and the implication is that even the covenant people need cleansing. The leaders feel the insult. By what authority?

John's answer is the center of the whole passage. Verse twenty-six. I baptize with water, but among you stands one you do not know. The verb is hesteken, stands. Present, perfect. He has taken his stand. He is already there, in the crowd, in the line, and nobody notices him. The Cambridge Bible catches how John sees himself in relation to him, saying that all the while, quote, He Who is far more than credentials to me is among you. End quote. Credentials. John has no credentials, and he does not need any, because the one who stands behind him is more than a reference.

Verse twenty-seven. Even he who comes after me, the strap of whose sandal I am not worthy to untie. Untying a sandal was the work of a household slave. John says, I am not fit even to be that. It is a small image with a long reach. The man who drew a nation to the river says he is not worthy to be the servant of the one who is coming.

Here is the application, and it comes straight from the text. Most of us do not have a crowd, but we all have a way we want to be known. John shows you the better question. Not, who am I, but who am I pointing to? A voice is only as good as what it says. So in your conversations today, in your home or at your work, see whether anyone can walk away having heard you, and still have been pointed toward him. Make yourself a voice.

Now to James.

Yesterday James opened with trials. Count it all joy when you meet trials of various kinds. Today he comes to the end of that thought and moves on to what happens in a trial, and where it goes wrong. Verse twelve. Blessed is the man who remains steadfast under trial, for when he has stood the test he will receive the crown of life, which God has promised to those who love him.

Blessed here is the same word as in the Beatitudes, and the crown is the stephanos, the wreath of the winner in the games, not a king's crown but a victor's. It is called the crown of life. Life is the prize, and the condition is love. Those who love him. Charles Spurgeon, in his notes on James, ties the verse to this. He writes that the purpose of our trials is, quote, that we should be made to love him more, and love him better. End quote. Trials do not earn the crown. They deepen the love that already holds it.

Now there is a subtle thing in the Greek. The word peirasmos can mean a trial or a temptation. A test from outside, or a pull from inside. It is the same word. In verses two through four it means testing. In verses thirteen and fourteen it means temptation to sin. James moves from one meaning to the other deliberately, and the shift is the whole point of the next few verses. A trial can be used by God to strengthen. But a temptation to do evil never comes from him.

Verse thirteen. Let no one say when he is tempted, I am being tempted by God, for God cannot be tempted with evil, and he himself tempts no one. People have always wanted to say it. Adam said, the woman whom you gave me. Anyone who has done wrong has wanted to hand the responsibility upward. James shuts that door. John Gill makes a quiet and rather profound point. He says no one can tempt another, quote, unless he is sinful himself. End quote. God is holy, so he cannot be the source of a pull toward evil.

Matthew Henry puts it with an image from the refiner's fire. He says that God, quote, is not the author of the dross, though his fiery trial exposes it. End quote. Dross is the scum that floats to the top of melted metal. The fire does not make it. The fire only shows that it was there. A hard circumstance does not put sin in you. It reveals what was in you.

And verse fourteen says where it comes from. But each person is tempted when he is lured and enticed by his own desire. Two fishing words. Lured means dragged out, as a fish is pulled from its hiding place. Enticed means baited. Your own desire is the hook and the bait together. Spurgeon says, quote, This is the essence of an evil temptation, a man's own lust. End quote. Lust there means desire gone bad. Not the outside circumstance but the inside appetite.

Then verse fifteen, a chain with the speed of a pregnancy. Desire, when it has conceived, gives birth to sin, and sin when it is fully grown brings forth death. Conceived. Birth. Fully grown. Death. James is describing something with a life cycle. It starts small and private, a wish you let linger, and ends as something that kills. This is not a warning for the monstrous. It is a warning for the ordinary mind at its ordinary work.

Verse sixteen is just five words. Do not be deceived, my beloved brothers. And then, in verse seventeen, comes the turn. Every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, coming down from the Father of lights with whom there is no variation or shadow due to change.

Look at what James has done. After the long picture of desire giving birth to death, he swings to a very different picture of what comes down from above. From the inside, the pull toward sin. From above, the gift. These are opposite directions. Everything good comes down, and no good thing is something God withholds or dangles.

The Father of lights. Light is plural, the lights, the sun and moon and stars. The Cambridge Bible explains that the plural expresses the thought that light in all its forms comes from him. And the next phrase fits: no variation or shadow due to change. The sun has shadows. As the earth turns, light moves and darkness creeps. The heavenly lights vary. The Cambridge Bible says it nicely. Quote. They, St James seems to say, have their changes, but not so their Creator and their Father. End quote. Spurgeon preached on this verse and prayed it, with the old Puritan prayer. Oh, for more light, he said, quote, from the great Father of lights. End quote. That is the whole prayer, and you could pray it this morning.

Verse eighteen is the most surprising. Of his own will he brought us forth by the word of truth, that we should be a kind of firstfruits of his creation. Brought us forth. It is the same verb as in verse fifteen, the verb for giving birth. Desire gives birth to sin and then to death. God gives birth to us by the word of truth. Two births, two mothers. One produces death, and the other life. And firstfruits means the first part of the harvest, the sign that more is coming. We are the first sheaf, and the whole creation will follow.

Notice that this is the same message we met yesterday in John. Not of the will of man, but of God. Now it is here again. Of his own will he brought us forth.

Now, the readings meet more than you might think, and I want to name just one real connection. The king in Ecclesiastes reaches out for everything his eyes desire. James tells us where that road leads when desire is in charge, and he also tells us where the good things actually come from. The Preacher got his portion by taking, and he found it was a breath. James says the good things are given, from the Father who does not shift. The king had everything except the one thing he could not seize, and John the Baptist had nothing and pointed to the one who stood among the people.

So here is the question to carry through your day. What are you reaching for today that you could receive instead? And is there someone nearby that you could point to?

In James, chapter one, verses nineteen through twenty-seven, quick to hear, slow to speak, and doers of the word. And in Ecclesiastes two, verses seventeen through twenty-six, the king hates his labor, and then finds that enjoyment itself is from the hand of God.
