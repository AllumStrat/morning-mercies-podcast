He acted as if he were going farther.

That is the line I cannot get past this morning. Two tired travelers have walked a whole afternoon with a stranger. They have heard him open up every book of Moses and the Prophets. They have reached their door. And Luke tells us that the stranger kept walking, as if there were somewhere else he needed to be. Think about that. The risen Lord of the universe, standing in a dusty road, waiting to be asked.

Good morning, Grace. This is Morning Mercies, and today is plan day nine twenty-three. We have three readings. Proverbs twenty-nine, verses fifteen through twenty-seven. Luke twenty-four, verses twenty-eight through thirty-five. And Hebrews twelve, verses fourteen through twenty-nine.

Yesterday we walked the first half of that Emmaus road with Cleopas and his friend, and we heard them say, we had hoped. We also heard Hebrews call the Christian life a race run under a Father's discipline, and Proverbs warn about the neck that will not bend. Today the road reaches its destination. The stranger is about to be recognized, and the stiff neck of Proverbs has a surprising counterpart in the table of an ordinary house. Hebrews, meanwhile, takes us from a table in a village house to a mountain that cannot be touched and a kingdom that cannot be shaken.

Let us begin with Proverbs.

Proverbs twenty-nine, verse fifteen. The rod and reproof give wisdom, but a child left to himself brings shame to his mother. Verse seventeen says it again from the parent's side. Discipline your son, and he will give you rest; he will give delight to your heart.

I want you to hear what kind of sentence this is. It is a proverb about cause and effect, the way a farmer knows that an untended field grows thistles. It is not a rulebook for every household. But it is honest about something we prefer not to say, which is that love and correction are not enemies. John Gill, the Baptist commentator, describes the child in verse fifteen this way: a child that has the reins thrown upon his neck, is under no restraint of parents. Notice the picture. A horse with the reins thrown loose is not a free horse. It is a horse nobody is steering. And Gill points out why the mother is named. She is generally the one most fond and most indulgent, and so she carries the shame.

Now verse eighteen is the most quoted verse in the section, and usually quoted wrongly. Where there is no prophetic vision the people cast off restraint, but blessed is he who keeps the law. You may have heard the older English, where there is no vision, the people perish, used to talk about a church needing a five year plan. That is not what the verse is about. The word vision here is chazon, the word for a revelation from God, the word used for the prophecies of Isaiah. The Pulpit Commentary notes that the verb means, in its own words, rather, cast off restraint, become ungovernable, cannot be reined in. So the proverb is not saying a nation needs a mission statement. It is saying that when God stops being heard, people stop being held. And the second half is the cure. Blessed is he who keeps the law. Not the one who admires it, or has it on a shelf, but who keeps it.

Notice how that verse sits right between the two verses on discipline. Verse seventeen says correct your son. Verse nineteen says that by mere words a servant is not disciplined, for though he understands, he will not respond. The chapter is circling one problem. Some people hear and understand and still do not move. Verse twenty then says it of the tongue. Do you see a man who is hasty in his words? There is more hope for a fool than for him. A fool, at least, knows he knows nothing. The hasty man has answered before he has heard.

Verse twenty-two returns to anger, verse twenty-three to pride. One's pride will bring him low, but he who is lowly in spirit will obtain honor. Gill says pride brought Adam low, and then Pharaoh and Nebuchadnezzar and Herod. Pride is a very old way to lose.

Verse twenty-four is a strange one, and it is worth slowing down for. The partner of a thief hates his own life; he hears the curse, but discloses nothing. The background is in Leviticus five, verse one. When something was stolen, the victim could call down a public curse on the thief and on anyone who knew about it and stayed quiet. The Pulpit Commentary explains that a witness who heard that curse and said nothing is in a double trap, because he becomes a liar on top of a bystander, and one sin leads to another. The proverb says that man hates his own life. He thinks he is saving himself by staying quiet. He is doing the opposite.

And that leads straight into the verse I want you to carry today. Verse twenty-five. The fear of man lays a snare, but whoever trusts in the Lord is safe. Matthew Henry, the Presbyterian, applies it to the most important place we could stay quiet. Many are ashamed to own Christ now; and he will not own them in the day of judgment. But he that trusts in the Lord will be saved from this snare. Look at the man in verse twenty-four. Why does he stay silent? He is afraid of men. And now look at the Christian who keeps his faith to himself at work. Same snare, smaller stakes.

Verse twenty-six says many seek the face of a ruler, but it is from the Lord that a man gets justice. Pay attention to where you are spending your effort to be noticed. And the chapter closes with the line that holds the whole section together. An unjust man is an abomination to the righteous, but one whose way is straight is an abomination to the wicked. The Pulpit Commentary explains why the upright man is hated, and it is not complicated: because he is a standing reproach to him.

So here is the practical question for today. Whose face are you watching? Is there one conversation this week you are rehearsing in your head, where you are more worried about how it will land than whether it is true? The proverb says that is a snare. It does not say the trap is loud. It says it is set, and it waits.

Now Luke.

Luke twenty-four, verse twenty-eight. We are at Emmaus, a village, Luke has told us, about sixty stadia from Jerusalem, around seven miles. It is evening. The two travelers reach the house, and Jesus acts as if he were going farther. Do not let the English mislead you. Alexander MacLaren, the great Baptist preacher of Manchester, says this was not play acting. The verb simply means he moved on as a stranger would, and he would have kept moving unless asked. Why? MacLaren answers that he wanted to draw out their request. And then comes one of the sentences I would put on a card by the door. Jesus Christ will certainly leave us if we do not detain Him.

Verse twenty-nine. They urged him strongly. The Greek word is parebiasanto, and it is a strong word. It means they pressed him, almost compelled him. Stay with us, for it is toward evening and the day is now far spent. Think about who is saying this. Three hours ago these two had written the whole story off. We had hoped, they said. Now they are begging a stranger to come in. Something happened to them on the road, and they cannot name it yet. They only know they are not ready to let him go.

Verse thirty. When he was at table with them, he took the bread and blessed it and broke it and gave it to them. Now I want to be careful here, because this verse has been fought over. John Calvin notes that Augustine, and the greater part of other commentators along with him, took this to be the Lord's Supper. Calvin disagrees. He thinks Luke is simply describing the ordinary thanksgiving of a Jewish meal, the kind Jesus gave before every meal. And Calvin draws a very plain lesson from that. As he puts it, let us learn by the example of our Master, whenever we eat bread, to offer thanksgiving to the Author of life. I will say as a Presbyterian that I side with Calvin here, and I know many of my Baptist friends read it his way as well. That reversal is the first surprise of the meal. The guest picks up the bread. And they have seen that gesture before.

Verse thirty-one. And their eyes were opened, and they recognized him. And he vanished from their sight.

Here is the hardest question in the passage. Why did they not know him earlier? Luke has told us their eyes were kept from recognizing him. And why does he disappear the moment they do?

Calvin gives a careful answer. He says Christ did not change shape, as though he were some Proteus in a Greek story. Instead, in Calvin's words, the eyes of beholders were mistaken, because they were covered. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, says the same thing in plainer language. He withdrew the veil from their eyes, which alone hindered their discerning him before, for the object was visible, only the medium of their sight was indisposed. In other words, Jesus was always the same Jesus. It was the seeing that had to be given.

And why the vanishing? Calvin offers a reason I find moving. The disciples, he says, were naturally too much addicted to the earth, and they might have wished to drag him back into the ordinary life they had known. So he gives them what they need, which is proof that he is risen, and then he removes the thing they would have clung to. The point is not that they should stop looking for him. It is that they should learn where to look.

Verse thirty-two. They said to each other, Did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road, while he opened to us the Scriptures? What I want to look at today is what Calvin sees in the moment of recognition. He says the disciples had felt a warmth that they had not recognized at the time. They now consider the grace which they had formerly, as it were, swallowed without tasting it, and perceive that they were stupid. Have you ever been through a stretch of your life where, looking back, you can see that God was working the whole time? You were being held, and you did not feel it. Calvin says that is common. God works in his people, he says, in such a way that for a time they are not aware of it.

Notice what they do next. Verse thirty-three. They rose that same hour and returned to Jerusalem. Remember they had just begged him to stay because the day was far spent. It is a seven mile walk in the dark. They do not wait until morning. Luke does not mention exhaustion, or fear of the road. When the Lord shows himself to you, the evening stops mattering.

They arrive and find the eleven, and before they can say a word they are met with news. The Lord has risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon. That is the only record in Luke of the appearance to Peter. Paul gives it one line in First Corinthians fifteen, verse five. We are told nothing of what was said. I think the silence is a kindness. Whatever passed between the Lord and the man who had denied him, it was not for us to overhear.

Then the two tell their own story, and Luke gives it in a single line. They told what had happened on the road, and how he was known to them in the breaking of the bread. Matthew Henry, in his short commentary on this passage, makes an observation I want you to keep. They had found the preaching powerful, even when they knew not the preacher.

So here is the application, and it is a small one. Today, at some point when the day starts to wind down, say the words of verse twenty-nine. Stay with us, for it is toward evening. Say them at the start of your reading, or at supper, or before you sleep. MacLaren says Jesus is glad to be forced, and the whole passage bears that out. He is not holding back. He walks as far as the door and waits to hear whether you want him in.

Now Hebrews.

We are in the closing exhortation of the letter. The writer has spent eleven chapters showing that Christ is better than the angels, than Moses, than the priests, than the sacrifices. Chapter twelve begins the pastoral turn: because of all this, run, endure, accept the Father's discipline. And now, in verse fourteen, the writer gets very practical. Strive for peace with everyone, and for the holiness without which no one will see the Lord.

Two things are tied together here, peace and holiness, and the verb strive is the word for pursuing, the same word used for chasing something. John Chrysostom, the fourth century preacher of Constantinople, opens his homily on this verse with a line I like very much. There are many things characteristic of Christianity: but more than all, and better than all, Love towards one another, and Peace. That is a church father telling the church to be known for its peace. And notice that peace alone is not enough. Holiness has to go with it, because peace without holiness is only comfort.

Then comes the line that troubles people. The holiness without which no one will see the Lord. Does that mean we earn our way to heaven by being good enough? Not in this letter, which has just spent chapters telling us that Christ has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified. John Piper handles the verse like this. He says, There is a practical holiness without which we will not see the Lord. Many live as if this were not so. That is the right balance. Holiness is not how you get in. It is the evidence that you are in, and it is the road that Christ's people walk. Hebrews does not say that holiness earns the sight. It says that no one will see the Lord without it, the way no one will see the sun without turning their face toward the morning.

Verse fifteen warns about the root of bitterness. That is a quotation from Deuteronomy twenty-nine, where a hidden root bears poisonous fruit in the camp. A bitter heart is rarely private for long. It springs up, says the writer, and by it many become defiled. If you are carrying something against a person, this verse says it will spread.

Then Esau. Verse sixteen. He sold his birthright for a single meal. And verse seventeen, the hard one. Afterward, when he desired to inherit the blessing, he was rejected, for he found no chance to repent, though he sought it with tears. Chrysostom says what Esau's sin was. He was profane, that is, gluttonous, without self-control, worldly, selling away things spiritual. And he says something about the tears that is worth noting. Esau's weeping was not repentance. His tears, Chrysostom says, were of pride and wrath. He was sorry about what the sale had cost him. He was not sorry that he had despised what was holy. The lesson is not that God refuses sinners who weep. Hebrews and the Gospels would contradict that on every page. The lesson is that some weeping is only grief at the consequences, and that is a very different thing.

Now the writer lifts our eyes. Verses eighteen through twenty-four are two mountains. You have not come to what may be touched, he says. Sinai, with its blazing fire and darkness and gloom and a tempest and the sound of a trumpet. The people begged that no further messages be spoken to them. Even Moses said, I tremble with fear. That is the mountain of the law, and there is no way across the line.

But you have come to Mount Zion. The writer piles up the names. The city of the living God, the heavenly Jerusalem, innumerable angels in festal gathering, the assembly of the firstborn who are enrolled in heaven, God the judge of all, the spirits of the righteous made perfect, and Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant. Notice the tense. Not you will come. You have come. If you belong to Christ, you are already standing in that crowd. And the last thing named is the sprinkled blood that speaks a better word than the blood of Abel. Abel's blood cried out from the ground for vengeance. Matthew Henry says of the blood of Christ, This blood of Christ speaks in behalf of sinners; it pleads not for vengeance, but for mercy.

And then the warning, and it is a serious one. See that you do not refuse him who is speaking. If Israel did not escape when they refused the voice on earth, how will we if we turn away from the voice from heaven? The writer quotes Haggai, who spoke of God shaking heaven and earth, and he takes the words Yet once more to mean the removal of the things that have been made, so that the unshakable things remain. Chrysostom asks the natural question. But of what sort are those things which cannot be shaken? The things to come. The kingdom is the one thing that will stay when everything else is moved.

That brings us to the final verses, and the final surprise. Therefore let us be grateful for receiving a kingdom that cannot be shaken, and thus let us offer to God acceptable worship, with reverence and awe, for our God is a consuming fire.

That last phrase is quoted from Deuteronomy four, verse twenty-four, where Moses warns Israel against forgetting the covenant and making idols. The Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges points out that Moses used it against apostasy and idolatry, so it is not a summary of everything God is. Gill pushes the other way and wants us to hear the word our. For here it is our God, our covenant God, our God in Christ. The fire is real. It is the fire of a God who will not share his glory. But for those who belong to him, Gill adds, that same fire guards and defends.

So the letter ends this section with two words side by side, gratitude and reverence. Notice that they are not opposites. The more clearly you see what you have received, the more carefully you will treat it.

Now, the close.

There is a real thread through all three readings, and I think you can feel it. In Proverbs, God's voice is the vision, and where it is not heard, people cast off restraint. In Luke, a voice speaks on the road and the two travelers do not know whose it is until they ask him to stay. And in Hebrews, the writer says, do not refuse him who is speaking. In all three, the Lord is the one who speaks, and the question is whether we will hold on to him or let him walk on.

Here is a question to carry through the day. When the Lord makes as though to go farther, what do you say to him?

Hebrews thirteen, verses one through eight, the last chapter of the letter, with its practical commands and its famous line about Jesus Christ being the same yesterday and today and forever. And Proverbs thirty, the words of Agur.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
