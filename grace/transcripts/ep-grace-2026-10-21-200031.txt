A man is walking into Jerusalem from the fields. It is early. He has come a long way to be in the city for the feast, and he has plans for the day. Then a soldier's hand closes on his shoulder, and a beam of rough wood is dropped across his back, and he is pushed into line behind a condemned man who can barely stand.

Behind them, the women of the city are wailing. And the condemned man stops, turns around, and tells them not to weep for him.

Here is the question to hold onto this morning. Is it possible to cry real tears about Jesus, and still miss him?

Welcome to Morning Mercies. This is the very first episode, so let me tell you what this is. Every morning, this show walks with you through the day's readings in the Navigators Bible Reading Plan. You have read the passages already, or you are about to, so my job is not to summarize them. My job is to help you see them more clearly: where we are in each book, what the words mean, what the hard questions are, and what faithful teachers across the centuries have said about them. Fathers of the early church, Reformers, Puritans, and pastors closer to our own day. Their words, quoted accurately, and the text first.

We are joining the plan in the middle of the year, at month nine, day sixteen. That is day two hundred and sixteen of three hundred. On this show we set the Old Testament track aside and keep three readings: a portion of the wisdom books, a Gospel, and the rest of the New Testament.

Today that means Proverbs twenty-six, verses one through sixteen, a gallery of fools and sluggards. Then Luke twenty-three, verses twenty-six through thirty-one, the road to the cross. And Hebrews nine, verses eleven through twenty-eight, where Christ enters heaven itself with his own blood. Luke is the heart of the episode today, but we will start in Proverbs.

Proverbs twenty-five, verse one, tells us where this part of the book comes from: "These also are proverbs of Solomon which the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied." So chapters twenty-five through twenty-nine are Solomon's sayings, gathered and arranged by scribes in Hezekiah's court around 700 B.C. And arranged is the right word, because our sixteen verses are not a random pile. They fall into two clear clusters.

Verses one through twelve are about the fool. The Hebrew word is kesil, and it shows up in every one of those verses except verse two. A kesil is not simply someone who is slow. The word describes a moral and spiritual stubbornness, a person who will not be taught. Then verses thirteen through sixteen turn to the sluggard, and every one of those four verses begins the same way: "The sluggard."

Notice how the poetry works. Most of these are comparison proverbs: "Like this, so that." And the comparisons are deliberately absurd. Verse one: "Like snow in summer or rain in harvest, so honor is not fitting for a fool." Snow in an Israelite summer is not just unusual, it is wrong. Rain at harvest ruins the grain. Honor on a fool does the same kind of damage. Verse seven: a proverb in a fool's mouth is like a lame man's legs, which hang useless. Verse nine is sharper: it is like a thorn in a drunkard's hand. He waves it around, and somebody gets hurt. The wisdom of these verses is in the pictures. Solomon wants you to smile, and then to wince.

Then we come to the hardest question in the passage, and it is famous. Verse four: "Answer not a fool according to his folly, lest you be like him yourself." Verse five: "Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes." Some of the ancient rabbis were so troubled by these two verses that they questioned whether Proverbs belonged in the Bible at all.

But the editors knew exactly what they were doing. The Puritan Matthew Poole asks how these contrary rules can be reconciled, and answers, "Easily, by considering the difference of persons, and times, and places, and other circumstances." Then he gets practical. Do not answer him, Poole says, "when he is incorrigible, or when he is inflamed with passion or wine." But do answer him "when he is capable of receiving good by it, or when it is necessary for the glory of God, or for the discharge of a man's duty, or for the good of others."

Proverbs is not a vending machine. It trains you to read the moment. R.C. Sproul, writing on these same verses, makes the point that the answer depends on the situation, and that verse five describes something like the old argument by absurdity: you take the fool's premise and follow it out until he can see where it leads.

You know this already. There is the late-night comment thread that you should simply close. And there is the friend at lunch who says something cynical about God, and who might actually be helped if you gently ask, "Where does that go, if it is true?" Same folly. Different moment.

Verse eleven gives us the most unforgettable image in the chapter: "Like a dog that returns to his vomit is a fool who repeats his folly." It is disgusting on purpose. The Apostle Peter quotes this proverb in Second Peter two, verse twenty-two, about people who seemed to escape the world's corruption and went back to it. Gregory the Great, the sixth-century bishop of Rome, picks it up in his Book of Pastoral Rule, in a chapter on people who lament their sins but never forsake them. He writes, "For the dog, when he vomits, certainly casts forth the food which weighed upon his stomach; but, when he returns to his vomit, he is again loaded with what he had been relieved from." Confession that leads straight back to the same sin does not leave you lighter. It loads you up again. Hold onto that. It is going to come back today.

Then verse twelve turns the whole chapter around. After eleven verses of mocking the fool, Solomon says: "Do you see a man who is wise in his own eyes? There is more hope for a fool than for him." The real danger is not the fool out there. It is the person who cannot imagine being the fool.

And that is where the sluggard comes in. Verse thirteen: "The sluggard says, 'There is a lion in the road!'" Lions lived in the wild country, not in town. The excuse is ridiculous, and it is meant to be. John Gill, the eighteenth-century Baptist pastor, says the slothful can always paint a lion, and that "a slight disorder of body, a small inclemency of the weather, little danger of catching cold, and the like, shall be a lion to them." The sluggard turns on his bed like a door on its hinges, verse fourteen. He is too tired to lift the spoon, verse fifteen. And verse sixteen ties it all together: "The sluggard is wiser in his own eyes than seven men who can answer sensibly." Laziness and self-conceit turn out to be the same disease.

So here is something practical for today. What is your lion? What duty have you been calling impossible that is really just unwelcome? Name it, and walk past it.

Now to Luke, and the heart of today's episode.

Luke twenty-three, verse twenty-five, has just told us that Pilate "delivered Jesus over to their will." Luke has been walking us toward this road for fourteen chapters. Back in chapter nine, verse fifty-one, Jesus "set his face to go to Jerusalem." In chapter nineteen he wept over the city. In chapter twenty-one he warned that armies would surround it. Now he is leaving it, on foot, under a sentence of death.

Verse twenty-six: "And as they led him away, they seized one Simon of Cyrene, who was coming in from the country, and laid on him the cross, to carry it behind Jesus."

Cyrene was a city in North Africa, in what is now Libya, with a large Jewish community. In the book of Acts, people from Cyrene show up in Jerusalem at Pentecost, and later as some of the first men to preach the Lord Jesus to Greeks in Antioch. Simon was probably a Jewish pilgrim in town for Passover. He was coming in from the field, and the Greek word Luke uses for what the soldiers did to him means they laid hold of him. Seized him.

If you have ever wondered how this fits with John's Gospel, which says Jesus went out bearing his own cross, Augustine worked through that question in his Harmony of the Gospels. His answer is simple: both happened. Jesus carried it as he went out, and on the way Simon was pressed into service for the rest of the distance.

And why would Roman soldiers give a condemned man any relief at all? Matthew Henry thought it was not kindness. They were afraid Jesus would collapse and die on the road, and rob them of the crucifixion. Even the help was cruel.

But look at Luke's last three words: "behind Jesus." John Piper points out that Luke is the only Gospel writer who says this. And it is hard to miss the echo. In Luke nine Jesus said, "If anyone would come after me, let him deny himself and take up his cross daily and follow me." In Luke fourteen, "Whoever does not bear his own cross and come after me cannot be my disciple." Here is a man literally doing it. And Simon did not choose the moment. It came on an ordinary morning, sudden and heavy and unwanted. Many of our crosses come the same way.

Mark adds that Simon was "the father of Alexander and Rufus," as if Mark's readers would know those two men. And in Romans sixteen, Paul greets a Rufus in Rome, "chosen in the Lord," and his mother, "who has been a mother to me as well." We cannot be certain it is the same family. Sinclair Ferguson suspects it is. If so, the worst interruption of Simon's life became the doorway of grace for his whole household.

Verse twenty-seven: "And there followed him a great multitude of the people and of women who were mourning and lamenting for him." These verses through thirty-one appear only in Luke. These women were not his Galilean disciples. They are daughters of Jerusalem, and their wailing was real. Jesus had taught daily in their temple. Now their own leaders had handed him over to die. Their grief made sense.

Which is why verse twenty-eight is so startling. "But turning to them Jesus said, 'Daughters of Jerusalem, do not weep for me, but weep for yourselves and for your children.'"

Here is the hard question. Why does Jesus turn away sympathy? Is he rebuking their tears?

Calvin says no, not exactly. He writes that Christ does not reprove them as if they wept without cause. He warns them that there will be far greater reason for weeping, because his death is not the end of Jerusalem's troubles but the beginning of them. Then verse twenty-nine: the days are coming when they will say, "Blessed are the barren." In that culture, childlessness was a grief. Jesus is saying that a day is coming so terrible that it will feel like mercy to have no children to watch suffer. It is the same warning he gave in chapter twenty-one, and it came true when Roman armies besieged Jerusalem in the year 70.

Verse thirty quotes the prophet Hosea. Hosea ten, verse eight, was a word of judgment against the northern kingdom and its idols: they will say to the mountains, cover us, and to the hills, fall on us. Jesus takes Hosea's words and applies them to Jerusalem. When people would rather be crushed by mountains than face what is coming, judgment has arrived.

Charles Spurgeon preached on this passage a hundred and fifty years ago this week, in October of 1876. He put it like this: "To weep over a dying Saviour is to lament the remedy; it would be wiser to bewail the disease." The cross is not the tragedy. Sin is the tragedy. The cross is the cure.

Then verse thirty-one, the most puzzling line in the passage: "For if they do these things when the wood is green, what will happen when it is dry?"

Here is the key Greek. The green wood is hugros, wood that is moist, still full of sap. The dry wood is xeros, withered and dead. Everyone who has built a campfire knows the difference. Green wood smokes and hisses and resists the flame. Dry wood goes up at once. Ezekiel twenty, verse forty-seven, had already pictured God's judgment as a fire that devours every green tree and every dry tree.

The commentators read the saying two ways. One reading: if the Romans treat the innocent like this, what will they do to a rebellious city? The other reaches deeper. R.C. Sproul preached it this way: "He was the green wood that normally you do not put in the fire to burn. He was not kindling for God's wrath. His wood was green. Our wood is dry."

Matthew Henry goes to the same place, and he puts it with real weight: "If God did this to the Son of his love, when he found sin but imputed to him, what shall he do to the generation of his wrath, when he finds sin reigning in them?"

Calvin allows both readings, and says either way the women's lament is foolish unless it is joined to a fear of God's judgment.

So here is the application, and it comes straight out of the text. It is good to be moved by the cross. Spurgeon himself said of these women, bless them for it. But being moved is not the same as being changed. Jesus did not want their pity. He wanted their repentance. And he was thinking about them, and their children, while he walked to his own death.

When you think about the cross today, do not stop at feeling sorry for him. Let it show you how serious your sin is. And then let it show you how much he loves you.

Now to Hebrews.

Hebrews chapters eight through ten are the center of the whole letter, the long argument that Jesus is the high priest every earlier priest was pointing to. The first ten verses of chapter nine described the earthly tent and the Day of Atonement, when once a year the high priest went alone behind the curtain with blood. Leviticus sixteen calls it atonement "once in the year because of all their sins." Our passage now sets Christ beside that picture, point by point.

Verse eleven: Christ came through "the greater and more perfect tent," not made with hands. Verse twelve: "he entered once for all into the holy places, not by means of the blood of goats and calves but by means of his own blood, thus securing an eternal redemption." The high priest went in every year. Christ went in once. Not into a room in Jerusalem, but into the presence of God.

Verses thirteen and fourteen make an argument from the lesser to the greater. Numbers nineteen describes the ashes of a red heifer mixed with water to cleanse someone who had touched a dead body. If that could make the flesh clean for worship, "how much more will the blood of Christ, who through the eternal Spirit offered himself without blemish to God, purify our conscience from dead works to serve the living God."

Dead works. What are those? John Chrysostom, the great fourth-century preacher, saw the connection to the red heifer immediately: "And well said he from dead works; if any man touched a dead body, he was polluted; and here, if any man touch a dead work, he is defiled through his conscience." Under the old law, contact with death made you unclean. Under the new covenant, the death we carry around is in our consciences: works done without life, without faith, without love, which leave a stain we cannot scrub out.

And you know what that stain feels like. John Piper asks it plainly: "When your conscience rises up and condemns you, where will you turn?" His answer, from this verse, is to turn "to the only cleansing agent in the universe that can give you relief in life, and peace in death." And notice where the verse ends. Not simply clean, but clean "to serve the living God." A washed conscience is not the finish line. It is what sets you free to serve.

Verses fifteen through twenty-two explain why a death was necessary. The writer plays on a Greek word, diatheke, which can mean a covenant or a last will. Calvin noticed that the Hebrew word for covenant has no such double meaning, which tells us this letter was written in Greek. A will only takes effect when the one who made it dies. So the promises of the new covenant are sealed by Christ's death. And even the first covenant began with blood: Moses sprinkled the people and said, as Exodus twenty-four records it, "Behold the blood of the covenant." Verse twenty-two sums up the whole system: "without the shedding of blood there is no forgiveness of sins."

Verse twenty-three draws the conclusion. The earthly tent was only a copy, and copies could be cleansed with the blood of animals. But the real thing, the presence of God himself, required "better sacrifices than these." And notice that the writer does not say Christ offered himself in some ordinary human strength. Verse fourteen said he did it "through the eternal Spirit." Calvin takes that to mean the worth of Christ's death is measured not by the outward act, but by the power of the Spirit, which is why its effect is eternal. A priest offering a goat could do nothing more than his own hands could do. This priest's offering reaches heaven.

Then the climax. Verse twenty-four: Christ entered "into heaven itself, now to appear in the presence of God on our behalf." Calvin is lovely on this verse. He writes that "it is Christ who really presents himself before God, and stands there to obtain favor for us, so that now there is no reason why we should flee from God's tribunal, since we have so kind an advocate, through whose faithfulness and protection we are made secure and safe." Grace, right now, as you listen, someone is standing in the presence of God on your behalf.

Listen to how often the writer uses one little word in these last verses. The Greek word is hapax, once. He appeared once at the end of the ages to put away sin, verse twenty-six. Man is appointed to die once, verse twenty-seven. Christ was offered once, verse twenty-eight. The repetition is the argument. It is finished. Nothing can be added to it.

Verses twenty-seven and twenty-eight set two certainties side by side: "just as it is appointed for man to die once, and after that comes judgment, so Christ, having been offered once to bear the sins of many, will appear a second time, not to deal with sin but to save those who are eagerly waiting for him." "Bear the sins of many" is the language of Isaiah fifty-three, verse twelve: "he bore the sin of many." Calvin notes that the point of "many" here is the contrast with one. One man bearing the sins of a multitude.

And picture the Day of Atonement again. The high priest disappears behind the curtain, and the people stand outside, waiting, wondering whether the sacrifice has been accepted. Then he comes out. That is the picture behind verse twenty-eight. Our high priest has gone in. He will come out again. And the word for those who are waiting describes eager, leaning-forward expectation. Not dread of judgment. Hope.

Let me draw two threads together before we close, because these readings really do touch.

The first is about tears and washing that do not change anything. Proverbs gave us the dog returning to its vomit, and Gregory's warning about people who lament their sins but never leave them. Luke gave us the women of Jerusalem, weeping real tears on the road to the cross. Spurgeon feared that many of them forgot by the next day that they had wept at all. And Hebrews tells us what real cleansing does: the blood of Christ purifies the conscience in order to serve the living God. Calvin says it bluntly. We are not washed by Christ so that we can plunge back into the filth. We are washed so that our lives can glorify God.

The second thread is judgment. On the road, Jesus spoke of fire, green wood and dry. In Hebrews, the writer says that every one of us will die once and then face judgment. But between those two warnings stands the cross, where the green wood went into the fire, once, to bear the sins of many. If you belong to him, the judgment you deserved has already fallen on him.

So here is a question to carry through your day. Where are you feeling sorry about something in your life, without actually turning from it? Bring that to the one who stands before God for you.
