Touch me and see.

Think about how strange that sentence is. Three days ago these men watched their teacher die. They have locked the doors. And now he is standing in the room, and the first thing he does is invite them to put their hands on him. Not to believe harder. Not to feel something. To touch.

Faith, in the Bible, is never a leap away from the evidence. It is a response to a person who has stood in front of you and said, look at my hands.

Welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day nine twenty-four, day two hundred twenty-four of three hundred. Today we read Proverbs thirty, the strange and wonderful words of a man named Agur. Luke twenty-four, verses thirty-six through forty-four, where the risen Jesus stands among the eleven. And Hebrews thirteen, verses one through eight, the first practical commands of the letter's last chapter.

Last time, you will remember, two travelers on the Emmaus road begged a stranger to stay with them, and their eyes were opened when he broke the bread. Today we find out where they went next. They ran seven miles back to Jerusalem in the dark, and they walked straight into the middle of the very scene we are about to read. And Hebrews has just told us that our God is a consuming fire. Today the writer shows us what a people who have received an unshakable kingdom actually do on Monday morning. They love, they open their doors, and they stay content.

Let's begin with Agur.

Proverbs thirty is unlike anything around it. For twenty-nine chapters we have heard Solomon and the men of Hezekiah. Now a new voice speaks, and he does not sound like a king. The chapter opens, the words of Agur, son of Jakeh, an oracle. The Hebrew word is massa, which means a burden or a weighty utterance, the same word the prophets use. Some translators read it as a place name, so Jakeh of Massa. Gill is honest about how little we know. He says, Who this Agur was is a matter of doubt. We do not know the man. We know only that his words were weighty enough to be kept inside the book of Proverbs, and that the Spirit placed them here.

And listen to how he introduces himself. I am weary, O God. I am too stupid to be a man. I have not learned wisdom, nor have I knowledge of the Holy One. Then comes a string of questions. Who has ascended to heaven and come down? Who has gathered the wind in his fists? Who has established all the ends of the earth? What is his name, and what is his son's name? Surely you know.

That is not false modesty. Agur is doing what Job did at the end of his book. He is standing under the sky and admitting that the creature cannot out-think the Creator. The whole chapter hangs on that admission. The beginning of wisdom is not cleverness. It is knowing where your knowledge stops.

Then, in verse five, Agur gives us the one thing we can stand on. Every word of God proves true; he is a shield to those who take refuge in him. Gill, commenting on the purity of the word, writes, The whole of Scripture is pure, free from all falsehood and error. Notice what happens next. Agur has just said he cannot see to the end of things. So he does not invent. He says, do not add to his words, lest he rebuke you and you be found a liar. Where our reason runs out, God's word does not. The humble man is not the one who has no convictions. He is the one who stops exactly where God stops speaking.

Now the prayer, in verses seven through nine. Two things I ask of you; deny them not to me before I die. Remove far from me falsehood and lying; give me neither poverty nor riches; feed me with the food that is needful for me, lest I be full and deny you and say, Who is the Lord? Or lest I be poor and steal and profane the name of my God.

Look at the logic. Agur is afraid of both ends of the income scale, and he is afraid of them for the same reason, what they do to his worship. Too much, and he may not feel his need. He will say, who is the Lord? Too little, and desperation may drive him to take what is not his, and to drag God's name through the mud. Matthew Henry says, Agur wisely prayed for a middle state, that he might be kept at a distance from temptations. And Matthew Poole notes why poverty ranks as a danger, poverty being commonly the occasion and temptation to the sin of lying. Neither man is calling poverty noble or wealth evil. Both are saying that your circumstances press on your soul, and a wise person asks God to arrange them with that in mind.

Hold on to that prayer, because Hebrews is about to hand it back to us.

The rest of the chapter is a collection of numbered sayings, and they have a famous pattern. Three things, even four. The leech has two daughters, Give and Give. Three things are never satisfied, and four never say, Enough. Greed has a voice, and Agur has heard it. Think about what it says that the chapter names greed as a creature with a mouth. The leech, the grave, the barren womb, the land, and the fire. Each of them keeps taking and never says it is full. A heart that wants more is in strange company. Then the things too wonderful to understand, the way of an eagle in the sky, a serpent on a rock, a ship on the sea. Then the way of an adulteress, who eats, wipes her mouth, and says, I have done no wrong. That is a sharp little portrait of a conscience that has simply stopped speaking.

And there are the four small creatures in verses twenty-four through twenty-eight. Ants, who store food in summer. Rock badgers, who make their homes in the cliffs. Locusts, who have no king yet march in ranks. And the lizard, which you can catch in your hands and yet it is found in kings' palaces. Barnes calls them, the marvels of instinct, which, in their own province, transcend the more elaborate results of human wisdom. Four weak things, and each one has what it needs. The ant plans. The badger finds shelter. The locust cooperates. The lizard gets in the door. Agur has just told us he is the stupidest of men, and then he sits down and learns from insects.

The last two verses give you your instruction for today. If you have been foolish, exalting yourself, or if you have devised evil, put your hand on your mouth. For pressing milk produces curds, pressing the nose produces blood, and pressing anger produces strife. Before you say the thing that cannot be unsaid, close your hand over your lips. It is the shortest lesson in the book of Proverbs, and probably the hardest.

Now to Luke.

You have to picture the room. It is Sunday evening, the day of the resurrection. Cleopas and his companion have just burst in from Emmaus and heard, the Lord has risen indeed, and has appeared to Simon. We are never told what Jesus said to Peter in that private meeting. Luke keeps it for them. And as they are all talking, verse thirty-six says, Jesus himself stood among them, and said to them, Peace to you.

Peace to you is the ordinary Hebrew greeting, shalom. But hear who is saying it. These are men who ran, who denied, who hid. Bede, preaching on this verse in his homilies on the Gospels, notes that the Lord condescended to stand in the middle of his disciples, and I think that is exactly right. He does not stand in front to scold them. He stands in the middle. And the first word out of his risen mouth to the men who failed him is peace.

It does not work. Verse thirty-seven, they were startled and frightened and thought they saw a spirit. The Greek word is pneuma. Ghost is the natural fear. Dead men do not walk through locked doors on Sunday, and these men have a dead friend. Jesus responds with two questions. Why are you troubled, and why do doubts arise in your hearts? Then the invitation. See my hands and my feet, that it is I myself. Touch me and see. For a spirit does not have flesh and bones as you see that I have.

I want you to stay on that for a moment, because it is the center of Christian belief about the resurrection. Jesus does not say, I am here in spirit. He says the opposite. The church has always heard it this way. Ignatius of Antioch, writing within a lifetime of the apostles, puts the invitation in his own letter to the church at Smyrna, with these words, Lay hold, handle Me, and see that I am not an incorporeal spirit. And Gill gives the reasoning in one line. Sight might be deceived, but feeling could not. Seeing can be tricked. A hand on a wrist cannot.

And notice the scars. He could have risen with a flawless body. He rose with his wounds. The risen Christ is not a better, cleaner version of the man who died. He is the same man, with the marks of what he did for us still in his hands. We will come back to that when we get to Hebrews.

Then comes my favorite line in the passage. Verse forty-one. While they still disbelieved for joy and were marveling, he said to them, Have you anything here to eat?

Disbelieved for joy. Ellicott captures the feeling, What is brought before their eyes is too good to be true. Spurgeon preached on this very phrase on May twenty-fifth, 1890, and he describes the whiplash exactly. You first rejoice because you believe it and then you begin to doubt it because you rejoice! Have you ever been there? You get news so good that you refuse to trust it, because if it turned out to be false, you could not bear the fall. That is not unbelief of the hard heart. That is the unbelief of a wounded one. And Jesus does not rebuke it. He asks for lunch.

They give him a piece of broiled fish, and he took it and ate before them. Some later manuscripts add a honeycomb, and Gregory the Great, in his homilies on the Gospels, found a sermon in it. For a honeycomb is honey in wax, but honey in wax is divinity in humanity. Whether or not Luke wrote the honeycomb, Gregory's point stands. A body that eats fish is a real body. The resurrection is not the soul escaping from the body. It is the body being raised.

Why does Luke work so hard on this? Because the whole Gospel is about eyewitness. In his preface he says he wrote what was handed down by those who were eyewitnesses from the beginning. Peter would later preach in Acts that they ate and drank with him after he rose from the dead. This is not a vision. It is evidence, handed to people who were looking for a reason to stay afraid.

Now the hard part. Verse forty-four. These are my words that I spoke to you while I was still with you, that everything written about me in the Law of Moses and the Prophets and the Psalms must be fulfilled. That is the standard threefold way a Jew spoke of the whole Hebrew Bible, and Gill says that of the three, among which last stands the book of Psalms, and is put for the whole. The question is the one every Christian eventually faces. Where, exactly, does the Old Testament talk about a suffering, rising Messiah? Luke does not list texts here. He tells us only that Jesus opened their minds to understand the Scriptures. The verb is dianoigo, to open thoroughly. It is the same word Luke used of the burning hearts on the Emmaus road, when he opened to them the Scriptures. Matthew Henry says plainly why it all had to happen. Nothing had passed but what was foretold by the prophets, and necessary for the salvation of sinners.

I think that is the answer, and it is honest. The disciples did not understand because they could not, and not because the Scriptures were unclear. It took the risen Lord himself to open their minds. Spurgeon's text for that sermon runs right through the line that matters most. And He opened their understanding, that they might understand the Scriptures. If you sometimes read the Old Testament and wonder how any of it fits, that is not a failure on your part. It is the very thing he promised to do.

So the application is this. When you feel the pull of that joy that is too good to believe, do not hide it. Bring it to him as the eleven did. Put your finger in the hands, so to speak. Then ask him, today, to open your mind to what you read. Not to give you a new insight to post or perform, but to let you see him in the pages the way the eleven finally saw him in that room. That is a prayer he has already said yes to in advance.

Now Hebrews thirteen.

Here the letter changes pace. For twelve chapters the writer has argued that Christ is greater than angels, Moses, Aaron, and every earlier covenant. Now he stops arguing and starts commanding, in short bursts. These eight verses read like a list, but they are a portrait of a church that has understood the argument.

Verse one. Let brotherly love continue. The word is philadelphia, the love of siblings. Notice he does not say, begin. These Christians already love each other. They once endured a hard struggle, in chapter ten, and stood with prisoners. He just says, keep going.

Verse two. Do not neglect to show hospitality to strangers, for thereby some have entertained angels unawares. Hospitality in Greek is philoxenia, love of strangers. Chrysostom, preaching on this verse in his thirty-third homily on Hebrews, quotes the line and presses it. Be not forgetful of hospitality, for hereby some have entertained angels unawares. The writer has Abraham and Sarah in mind at their tent by the oaks, and perhaps Lot in Sodom. In a world with no hotels, a traveling Christian relied entirely on the homes of other believers. The Pulpit Commentary draws the lesson wider. At any time the visits even of our fellow-men may be to us as visits of angels. You will not know who is at your table. Treat everyone as though it might matter.

Verse three. Remember those in prison as though you were in prison with them, and those who are mistreated, since you also are in the body. The writer's own readers have been in jail. He says, imagine yourself in the cell. Empathy for the suffering is not optional equipment. Because you have a body, you can be hurt as they are.

Verse four. Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the marriage bed be undefiled, for God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterous. The writer puts marriage right beside the stranger and the prisoner as part of ordinary holiness. It is not a private matter outside the church's concern. And you may remember Agur's adulteress who wiped her mouth and said, I have done no wrong. Hebrews says God notices.

Then verse five, and here Agur's prayer comes back. Keep your life free from love of money, and be content with what you have, for he has said, I will never leave you nor forsake you. Notice the because. Contentment is not a mood you work yourself into. It rests on a promise. The quotation comes from Deuteronomy thirty-one, verse six, where Moses told Israel, and later God told Joshua, before the conquest. Barnes sees the reasoning clearly. The particular reason here given for contentment is, that God has promised never to leave his people. And the Greek stacks five negatives on top of each other. It is something like, I will never, no never, leave you, no, never forsake you. God does not hint here. He piles up the denials. Henry puts the result simply. Having treasures in heaven, we may be content with mean things here. Agur wanted neither poverty nor riches, only enough. Hebrews gives the reason enough is enough. The one thing that cannot be taken from you is the presence of God. Chrysostom says it like a pastor. You have the promise from Himself: do not doubt henceforward.

So we say with confidence, verse six, The Lord is my helper; I will not fear; what can man do to me? That is Psalm one hundred eighteen, quoted as a fixed sentence for the congregation.

Verse seven. Remember your leaders, those who spoke to you the word of God. Consider the outcome of their way of life, and imitate their faith. These are leaders who have died, or left, and the church is to remember how they finished. And then the sentence that makes sense of everything, verse eight. Jesus Christ is the same yesterday and today and forever.

Do not read this as a floating slogan. It is placed right after the leaders. Leaders come and go, the church buries them, and the one who gave them to the church does not change. Chrysostom unpacks the three words. Yesterday means all the time that is past: today, the present: for ever, the endless which is to come. The Cambridge Bible adds that the unchangeableness of Christ is a reason for not being swept about by winds of strange teaching, and that is exactly what verse nine goes on to say. Because he does not change, his gospel cannot be improved on.

And Spurgeon, preaching on this text in January of 1858, reaches for the tenderest application. He says of Christ's love, He can not love thee more; he will not love thee less. If you have ever wondered whether your failures have worn out his patience, that line answers you. The same Jesus who said peace to the eleven, who showed his scars and asked for fish, is the Jesus who is with you today, and he has not changed his mind.

Let me close.

There is a real thread today, and I think you can feel it. In Luke, the risen Lord stands among terrified friends, shows his hands, and says peace. In Hebrews, that same Lord is the same yesterday, today, and forever, so we can open our doors to strangers and let go of our money. And in Proverbs, a man who calls himself the dullest of men asks only for daily bread and the truth, because he has learned that God's word is a shield. Humility, presence, and enough. They are all one lesson.

Here is a question to carry through the day. If Jesus walked into your day and said, touch me and see, what would you still be afraid of?
