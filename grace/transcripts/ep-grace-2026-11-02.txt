Gone. Gone forever. Utterly abolished.

That is how Charles Spurgeon described what happened to your sin when Jesus carried it. And this morning, in the very same stretch of readings, a king in Jerusalem sits down at the end of a long career, looks at everything he built, and says he hates it. Two men, two verdicts on what the future holds for what we leave behind. Let's listen to both.

Good morning, Grace, and welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day three of month ten, and today we have three readings. In Ecclesiastes, chapter two, verses seventeen through twenty-six. In John, chapter one, verses twenty-nine through thirty-four. And in James, chapter one, verses nineteen through twenty-seven.

Yesterday we ended with a question. What are you reaching for that you could receive instead? The Preacher in Ecclesiastes had just finished his great experiment in reaching, and we said that pleasure is a gift and not a project. Today he comes back to that idea from the other side, and for the first time he says plainly where the gift comes from. And John the Baptist, who yesterday kept saying I am not, today finally says who is. Let's begin with the Preacher.

Ecclesiastes two, verses seventeen through twenty-six.

You will remember where we left him. The king has tried pleasure, wine, laughter, gardens, pools, servants, silver and gold. He has tried wisdom. And in verse sixteen he saw that the wise man and the fool both die and are both forgotten. Now he draws the conclusion, and it is a dark one. Verse seventeen. So I hated life, because what is done under the sun was grievous to me, for all is vanity and a striving after wind.

Hated life. That is strong language, and the Bible does not tidy it up for us. The word vanity is the Hebrew hebel, which means a breath, a vapor, the fog on a window. You can see it but you cannot hold it. And striving after wind is a man chasing something he can never catch. Notice that he is not saying life is evil. He says what is done under the sun is grievous. Under the sun is his phrase for life considered on its own, with God left out of the equation. His problem is not that he lacks things. His problem is that he cannot keep them.

Verse eighteen sharpens it. He hates all his toil, because he must leave it to the man who comes after him. And who knows whether that man will be wise or a fool? Yet he will control all that I toiled for and used my wisdom for under the sun.

The old Baptist commentator John Gill gets at the sting of this. The king, he says, hated his labor, quote, which a man cannot carry with him when he dies, but must leave all behind him. End quote. Think about what that is like. A man builds a business, a house, a name. He has poured decades into it. And on the day he dies, it goes to someone he did not choose, who may waste it in a month.

Now the tradition holds that this is Solomon speaking, and if so, the heir he has in mind is Rehoboam. First Kings twelve tells us what Rehoboam did with the kingdom. He listened to foolish counsel and split it in two. So the Preacher's fear was not abstract. He was looking down the road at a particular son.

Verses twenty through twenty-three show how deep this goes. He turns, he says, and gives his heart up to despair over all the toil. A man may work with wisdom and knowledge and skill, and then hand it to someone who did not work for it at all. All his days are full of sorrow, and his work is a vexation. Even in the night his heart does not rest. This is the man who cannot sleep. He is lying in the dark, and the ledger is running in his head.

If the passage ended there, it would be a counseling session for despair. But it does not end there. Verse twenty-four turns. There is nothing better for a person than that he should eat and drink and find enjoyment in his toil. This also, I saw, is from the hand of God.

This is the first time this refrain appears in the book. It will come back again and again, in chapters three, five, eight and nine. Eat, drink, enjoy your work, and see it as God's gift. Notice the key phrase. From the hand of God. Not from the work. Not from the wisdom. From a hand that gives.

Matthew Poole, the Puritan, says that this enjoyment is, quote, a singular gift of God, and not to be procured by a man's own wisdom or diligence. End quote. Read that slowly. You cannot earn the ability to enjoy. A man can build the palace and not be able to enjoy a single room of it. Gill says the same, that God gives not only the riches a man possesses but a heart to make use of them. The thing the king could not seize, no matter how hard he reached, was the capacity to rest in what he had. And that capacity is handed down.

Verse twenty-five asks, for who can eat and who can have enjoyment apart from him? Apart from God, in other words. Even a simple meal is a thing that only comes with him.

Now verse twenty-six is the hard one, and I want to be honest about it. To the one who pleases him, God gives wisdom and knowledge and joy, but to the sinner he gives the business of gathering and collecting, only to give to one who pleases God. Does that mean the righteous always get rich and the wicked always lose it? Not in this book. Ecclesiastes will say elsewhere that the righteous sometimes suffer and the wicked sometimes prosper. And notice that verse twenty-six ends the same way the chapter's earlier sections did. This also is vanity and a striving after wind. So the Preacher is not giving a formula. He is observing that the deepest goods, wisdom, knowledge, and joy, are not things any man can produce. They are given, and the one who receives them is the one who stands rightly before God. The sinner can heap up, but he cannot enjoy, and what he heaps up often lands in other hands.

So here is the practical turn for today. Take one ordinary thing, a meal, a cup of coffee, a conversation with someone you love, and instead of treating it as the reward you earned, receive it as something put into your hand. The Preacher says that is where joy begins. Not at the top of the ladder, but in the receiving.

Now to the Gospel, and a very different scene.

John, chapter one, verses twenty-nine through thirty-four. Yesterday we stood with the delegation from Jerusalem and heard the Baptist say what he was not. John's Gospel counts the days carefully. The questioning was one day, and verse twenty-nine begins, the next day. We are at Bethany beyond the Jordan, and the Baptist looks up and sees Jesus coming toward him.

And he says the words that have echoed for two thousand years. Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!

Stop on the word lamb. A Jewish listener would hear a dozen things at once. The Passover lamb, whose blood on the doorposts spared Israel's firstborn in Exodus twelve. The lamb Abraham told Isaac God would provide, in Genesis twenty-two. The lamb led silent to the slaughter in Isaiah fifty-three. The daily lamb offered morning and evening in the temple. John Calvin, in his commentary, says simply, quote, By the word Lamb he alludes to the ancient sacrifices of the Law. End quote. All of those lambs were pointing, and the Baptist says the pointing is over. Here he is.

Chrysostom, preaching on this verse in the fourth century, notices how final it is. He writes of the lamb of Moses, quote, That Lamb of Moses took not at once away the sin of any one; but this took away the sin of all the world. End quote. The old lambs were many, and they had to be repeated. This is one, and he is enough.

Now look at the verb. Takes away. In Greek it is airon, from airo, and it has a double sense. It can mean to lift up and to carry, and it can mean to remove. Both fit. He lifts the sin onto himself, and he carries it away. And the word sin is singular. Not sins, plural, a list of misdeeds, but sin as one great weight, the whole fallen condition. Calvin draws a sobering conclusion from the phrase, the sin of the world. Quote. But hence we infer that the whole world is involved in the same condemnation. End quote. No one stands outside it. The Baptist is not saying that all will be saved. He is saying that the Lamb's work is large enough for people from every nation, which is what Revelation will later sing.

Charles Spurgeon preached on this very verse in October of eighteen eighty seven, and he put the carrying and the removing together in one line. By bearing it, he said, he bore it away. Quote. It is gone, gone for ever, it is utterly abolished. End quote. That is where we began this morning. Not the sin managed, not the sin covered up, but gone.

And the Presbyterian R. C. Sproul gives the logic behind it. Preaching on this title of Christ, he said, quote, The idea of the atonement of Christ is that Christ stands as our substitute. End quote. A lamb dies, and someone else goes free. That is substitution, and it is the center of the gospel.

Verses thirty and thirty-one. The Baptist repeats what he said yesterday, that after him comes a man who ranks before him because he was before him. Then comes a puzzling line. I myself did not know him, but for this purpose I came baptizing with water, that he might be revealed to Israel.

I did not know him. But the Baptist and Jesus were relatives, and Luke tells us their mothers were kin. Surely he knew who Jesus was. The Cambridge Bible for Schools handles this well. It says, quote, I knew Him not need not mean I had no knowledge of Him whatever. End quote. John knew his cousin. What he did not know, until it was shown to him, was that this cousin was the Messiah. Chrysostom makes a lovely point about the tense. John says I knew him not, not I know him not now, and so, Chrysostom says, he, quote, would be shown most trustworthy. End quote. He is not a man who has cooked up a story with a friend. He is honestly reporting what he saw, and what he saw came from God.

So how did he know? Verses thirty-two and thirty-three. He saw the Spirit descend from heaven like a dove, and it remained on him. The one who sent John to baptize had told him that the man on whom he saw the Spirit descend and remain was the one who baptizes with the Holy Spirit.

Remained. That is the Greek meno, to stay, to abide. It is one of John's favorite words. The Spirit did not flit over Jesus like a bird that lands and takes off. He stayed. The prophets had the Spirit come on them for a time. Here the Spirit takes up residence. And the dove recalls the Spirit hovering over the waters in Genesis one, the first creation. This is the beginning of the new one.

Then verse thirty-four, the climax. I have seen and have borne witness that this is the Son of God. Seen and borne witness. John is a witness in the courtroom sense, and his testimony has two halves. This man takes away sin. This man gives the Spirit. First the Lamb, then the baptizer. He cannot give the Spirit to anyone until he has first dealt with their guilt, and the Gospel moves in just that order.

The hardest question here is a simple one. If the Lamb has taken away the sin of the world, why do I still feel it so much? Why does an old failure come back at three in the morning?

Here is how I would answer it. The Baptist does not say, feel the Lamb. He says, behold. Look. Faith in this passage is a looking. When the old guilt returns, the answer is not to grit your teeth and feel forgiven. It is to turn your eyes from the sin to the Lamb who carried it. Remember Spurgeon: it is gone, utterly abolished. The next time that accusation returns, say aloud what John said. Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world. Say it as many times as you need to. It is a command to look, and he never grows tired of being looked at.

Now to the third reading. James, chapter one, verses nineteen through twenty-seven.

You may remember that yesterday James told us that God brought us forth by the word of truth. That sets up everything in today's passage, because the question now is, what do we do with the word we have been given? The passage has three movements. How to hear it, verses nineteen through twenty-one. How to do it, verses twenty-two through twenty-five. And what doing it looks like in a real life, verses twenty-six and twenty-seven.

Verse nineteen. Know this, my beloved brothers. Let every person be quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger. The Greek has two opposite adjectives, tachys, quick, and bradys, slow, and they are set against one another. Quick to hear, slow to speak, slow to anger. The Cambridge Bible notes that the Jews had a proverb much like it, and says of one such old teacher, quote, To him speech was of silver, and silence of gold. End quote. But notice that James is not offering general advice about being a good listener. He is talking about how we receive the word of God. The anger he has in mind is the anger of the person who bristles when the word of truth presses on a sore spot. Calvin thinks the same. Wrath, he says, is condemned, quote, with regard to the hearing which God demands to be given to him. End quote.

Verse twenty. The anger of man does not produce the righteousness of God. A man's temper cannot do God's work. You may be right about the thing, but your fury will not make it righteous.

Verse twenty-one. Therefore put away all filthiness and rampant wickedness and receive with meekness the implanted word, which is able to save your souls. Put away, like taking off dirty clothes. Receive, like a guest at the door. And the word is implanted, planted like a seed in soil. The Greek is emphytos, rooted in. Calvin presses on the word. He says that James, quote, requires that it should be a living implanting, by which the word becomes as it were united with our heart. End quote. This is not a word that sits on the surface of your mind like dust on a shelf. It is a word that grows.

And it is able to save your souls. Remember, James is writing to people who are already brothers. So this is the saving word that carries a believer through life, not only at the beginning but all the way home.

Verse twenty-two is the hinge. Be doers of the word, and not hearers only, deceiving yourselves. Notice who is deceived. Not the world, and not the preacher. Yourselves. The hearer who does nothing has talked himself into thinking he has done something. Hearing felt like obeying.

Then comes the picture. A person who hears and does not do is like a man who looks intently at his natural face in a mirror. In the first century this would have been polished bronze. He looks, goes away, and immediately forgets what he was like. Spurgeon preached on this passage in eighteen seventy nine, and he gave it a very sharp edge. Looking in the glass and noticing a black mark on your forehead, he said, is, quote, mere child's play if you do not wash the spot away. End quote. A mirror does not clean you. It only tells the truth. And the word of God works the same way. It shows you what is there. What do you do with that?

Verse twenty-five gives the opposite picture. The one who looks into the perfect law, the law of liberty, and perseveres, being no hearer who forgets but a doer who acts, he will be blessed in his doing. The word James uses for looking here means to stoop and peer, as one bends down to look closely. And the law is perfect, and it is a law of liberty. Those two phrases belong together. God's word does not shackle the believer. It frees him to do what he was made to do. A fish is free in water, and the word is the water.

Then, verses twenty-six and twenty-seven, James brings it down to the street. If anyone thinks he is religious and does not bridle his tongue but deceives his heart, this person's religion is worthless. The Greek word for religion, threskeia, means outward observance, the visible practice of worship. James is not scorning worship. He is saying that real worship can be seen in two places. In a bridled tongue, and in the way we treat the vulnerable. Religion that is pure and undefiled before God the Father is this, to visit orphans and widows in their affliction, and to keep oneself unstained from the world.

Orphans and widows were the people in the ancient world with no one to speak for them. They had no legal standing and no income. To visit them means to go and look after them, and the word suggests a care that costs something. And unstained from the world means not letting the world's standards soak into you.

Notice how the passage has come full circle. It began with the tongue, slow to speak, and it ends with the tongue bridled. It began with receiving the word and ends with the word becoming action. The faith James describes is not noisy. It listens, it receives, it acts, and it is gentle.

So as we close, notice what the three readings share. The Preacher found that the good things of life, a meal, a day's work, wisdom, joy, are gifts from a hand he cannot command. John found that the hardest thing of all, the weight of sin, is not something we carry away ourselves. It is taken. And James says that the word we have received, we receive with meekness and then we do it. In each case the move is the same. Open your hands. The king reached and grasped and found wind. The Baptist pointed and found the Lamb. And James says, receive the implanted word.

So here is the question to carry through your day. Where have you heard the word clearly, and not yet done it? Pick one thing, one, and do it before the sun goes down.
