Stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high.

Those are nearly the last instructions Jesus gives before he leaves. Not go and conquer. Not go and prove yourselves. Stay. Wait. Be given something you cannot produce. And then, in the same breath, he lifts his hands and blesses them, and he is gone.

Have you ever noticed that the last thing the disciples see of Jesus on earth is not a sermon or a miracle? It is a blessing. Hands raised, words of grace, and then silence.

Welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day nine twenty-five, day two hundred twenty-five of three hundred. Today we read Proverbs thirty-one, the words of a king's mother and the famous poem about a woman who fears the Lord. Luke twenty-four, verses forty-five through fifty-three, where the risen Jesus opens the minds of his friends and ascends. And Hebrews thirteen, verses nine through twenty-five, the end of the letter, with its call to go outside the camp and its beautiful closing prayer.

Yesterday, you will remember, Jesus stood in the locked room and said, touch me and see, and he ate a piece of broiled fish in front of them. Today the same table talk continues. He has proved he is alive. Now he explains what it all means, and then he sends them out. And the book of Proverbs, which has been teaching us for weeks to get wisdom, comes to its last page. It ends not with a rule but with a portrait.

Let's begin with Proverbs.

Proverbs thirty-one has two halves, and they are easy to blur together. The first nine verses are the words of King Lemuel, and they are an oracle that his mother taught him. We do not know who Lemuel was. Some think the name is a poetic title for Solomon, and some think he was a king of a neighboring people. What we know is that the book closes with a mother talking to her son, and that tells you something about where the Bible thinks wisdom is handed down. It happens in a home, at close range, in a mother's voice.

Her advice is surprisingly practical. Do not give your strength to women, do not drink the wine that makes kings forget the law. Then comes the part that is easy to skip. Open your mouth for the mute, for the rights of all who are destitute. Open your mouth, judge righteously, defend the rights of the poor and needy. Notice the verb. She tells him twice to open his mouth. A king can ruin a kingdom by what he drinks, and he can ruin it just as thoroughly by what he refuses to say. Power is given so that someone who has no voice can have one.

Then the poem begins, and the form changes. Verses ten through thirty-one are an acrostic. Each verse begins with the next letter of the Hebrew alphabet, from aleph to tav, A to Z. That is a way of saying this is a complete picture. It is wisdom from beginning to end.

The first line asks, an excellent wife, who can find? The Hebrew phrase is eshet hayil. Hayil is a word you meet all over the Old Testament. It means strength, valor, capability. It is the word used for an army. It is used of Ruth, who is called a woman of hayil in the book of Ruth. So this is not a decorative word. It is not a picture of a woman who is pretty and pleasant. It is a picture of a woman with force.

And what does the force do? She works with willing hands. She gets up while it is still night. She considers a field and buys it. With the fruit of her hands she plants a vineyard. She makes cloth and sells it. She is a manager, a buyer, a seller, a seamstress, and a land investor. And in the middle of all of it, in verse twenty, she opens her hand to the poor and reaches out her hands to the needy. Do you see the connection to the first nine verses? Lemuel was told to open his mouth for the needy. She opens her hands.

Now here is a question worth asking. Is this a to-do list? Many women have read this poem and come away exhausted, as if God were asking for a perfect schedule. But look at how the poem ends. After thirty verses of energy, the climax in verse thirty is quiet. Charm is deceitful, and beauty is vain, but a woman who fears the Lord is to be praised.

Matthew Henry, on that verse, writes that, quote, the fear of God reigning in the heart, is the beauty of the soul; it lasts for ever. End quote. The Cambridge Bible for Schools and Colleges notices something about where we are in the whole book. It says, quote, Thus does Wisdom, true ever to herself, return in her last utterance to her first. End quote. Remember that Proverbs opened by saying the fear of the Lord is the beginning of knowledge. Here, on the last page, the fear of the Lord is still the thing that makes a life. All the competence in the poem flows from that one root. She is not valuable because she does so much. She does so much because she fears the Lord.

John Gill adds a plain warning about the things we chase, saying that, quote, natural or artificial beauty, are vain and deceitful. End quote. That has not aged. Beauty is real, and it is a gift, but it is a poor thing to build a life on.

There is one more voice to hear. John Piper preached on this very verse, and his emphasis is on how we treat a woman like this. He says, quote, Praise her, praise her, praise her while you have her. End quote. Notice that the poem agrees. In verse thirty-one, give her the fruit of her hands, and let her works praise her in the gates. Her husband and her children rise up and call her blessed. The wisdom here is for the one doing the praising as much as the one being praised. Who is it in your life that has been quietly doing the work, and when did you last say so out loud?

That is the practical edge of the chapter. Whatever else you take from this, take that the fear of the Lord, not busyness and not appearance, is what makes a person worth honoring, and that honoring them is your job.

Now, to Luke.

Luke twenty-four, verse forty-five. We are still in the same room, on the same evening of the same day. The two from Emmaus have run back and told their story. Jesus has appeared. He has shown them his hands and feet. He has eaten a piece of fish. In verse forty-four he reminds them that everything written about him in the Law of Moses, the Prophets, and the Psalms must be fulfilled.

And then verse forty-five. Then he opened their minds to understand the Scriptures.

Pause on that. These men had spent three years with Jesus. They had heard the sermons and watched the miracles. And they still did not get it. The Scriptures were open on the table, and their minds were closed. The problem was not a lack of information. The problem was something no amount of study could fix.

John Gill puts it this way. He says Jesus, quote, removed the veil from the eyes of their minds, and gave them an understanding of the sacred writings. End quote. And Matthew Henry makes the lesson universal. He writes, quote, Even good men need to have their understandings opened. End quote. I find that honest and comforting. If you have ever read a passage ten times and still felt that it was behind glass, you are in good company. The eleven were in that company too.

Albert Barnes states the same truth as a principle. He says the lesson is, quote, That God only can open the mind so as fully to comprehend the Scriptures. End quote. So before you open your Bible tomorrow morning, there is a short prayer worth praying. Lord, open my mind. Jesus did not hand the eleven a better study method. He did the opening himself, and he is still willing to do it for anyone who asks.

The Greek verb is dianoigo, and it means to open wide, to open completely. Luke has used it already in this chapter. In verse thirty-one, the eyes of the Emmaus travelers were opened. In verse thirty-two they say that he opened the Scriptures to them. Now the same verb is used for the mind. Jesus opens the Book, and he opens the person. You need both.

Then in verse forty-six he gives them the summary of the Old Testament. Thus it is written, that the Christ should suffer and on the third day rise from the dead. Verse forty-seven adds the next part, and that the forgiveness of sins should be proclaimed in his name to all nations, beginning from Jerusalem. Notice that the message is two events and one command. The suffering and rising are what happened. Repentance and forgiveness are what must be preached. And the scope is every nation. Matthew Poole, on the word repentance, calls it, quote, a turning from sinful courses into a course of life. End quote. Repentance is not a mood. It is a change of direction.

Why begin at Jerusalem? Because that is where the crime was committed, and that is where the mercy starts. The first people to hear that sins can be forgiven are the very city that had just crucified him. Hold that for a moment. Grace does not skip over the ones who did the most harm. It starts with them.

That should shape how you think about anyone who has wronged you, and about your own record as well. If the gospel begins with the people who did the worst, then no one you know is too far gone to be offered forgiveness, and neither are you.

Verse forty-eight says, you are witnesses of these things. A witness is not someone with a theory. A witness is someone who saw. Then verse forty-nine, the promise. I am sending the promise of my Father upon you. But stay in the city until you are clothed with power from on high. That is Luke preparing us for the book of Acts, which he will write next. The command to wait is real. The disciples cannot begin the mission in their own strength, and Jesus does not let them try.

John Calvin also saw the connection between the opening of the mind and the work of the Spirit. In his Harmony of the Gospels he ties this verse to the breathing of the Spirit in John twenty. I was not able to pull his exact words on this page today, so I will only say that Calvin treats understanding the Scriptures as a gift of the Spirit, not an achievement of the scholar. That is a good thing to remember on mornings when the reading feels dry.

Then the ending. Verse fifty. He led them out as far as Bethany, and lifting up his hands he blessed them. Bethany was the village of Mary, Martha, and Lazarus. It was a place where Jesus had been welcomed. Verse fifty-one. While he blessed them, he parted from them and was carried up into heaven.

The Greek says he was being carried up, in the middle of the blessing. The blessing does not end. It just goes upward with him. Matthew Henry says, quote, He did not go away in displeasure, but in love, he left a blessing behind him. End quote.

Charles Spurgeon preached on this passage in a sermon called Our Lord's Attitude in Ascending. He points to the hands themselves, hands that still carried the wounds. He says, quote, Thus they knew that they were Christ's hands. End quote. Think about that. The last thing the disciples see is the same pair of hands they touched in that locked room. Those hands are now raised in blessing. Spurgeon adds, quote, The blessing was the fit finis of His sojourn here; nothing fitter, nothing better, could have been thought of. End quote. Finis means the end. The visit began with a manger and ends with a blessing.

What do the disciples do? Verse fifty-two. They worshiped him and returned to Jerusalem with great joy. Great joy. Remember that this was a group that had been hiding behind locked doors. Their teacher has just left them, and they come back rejoicing. Why? Because they do not think he has gone away. They think he has gone up, and he is reigning, and he is coming back. Verse fifty-three says they were continually in the temple blessing God.

One application, and it is a simple one. Jesus' last act toward his people was to bless them. When you pray tonight, you are not talking to someone who is keeping a ledger of your failures. You are talking to the one whose last gesture on earth was raised hands. Come to him expecting a blessing, not a bill.

Now, to Hebrews.

We are at the end of the letter. Hebrews thirteen, verse nine. Do not be led away by diverse and strange teachings, for it is good for the heart to be strengthened by grace, not by foods, which have not benefited those devoted to them.

Why foods? We are not sure of the exact teaching, but the context is clear enough. Some in this community were being drawn back toward ritual meals and ceremonial rules, as if keeping them could secure a place with God. The writer says no. The heart is held up by grace. It is not held up by menus. The old sacrifices had food attached to them, and the worshipers ate their portions. But the new covenant has a better altar. Verse ten. We have an altar from which those who serve the tent have no right to eat.

Then comes the image that drives the rest of the chapter. In verse eleven, the writer recalls the Day of Atonement. The bodies of the animals whose blood was brought into the holy place were burned outside the camp. Verse twelve. So Jesus also suffered outside the gate in order to sanctify the people through his own blood. The old system took the blood inside and the carcass outside. Jesus is the sacrifice, and he was taken outside, to the place of the rejected, to the place of shame.

So verse thirteen. Therefore let us go to him outside the camp and bear the reproach he endured.

This is the heart of the passage. Go to him. If Jesus is outside, then that is where his people belong. The camp represents safety, belonging, respectability. For these readers it was Judaism with its temple and its protections. Outside meant rejection, possible loss of property, even loss of standing with family.

John Chrysostom explains the phrase directly. He reads it as, quote, Let us go forth therefore to Him without the camp, bearing His reproach, that is, suffering the same things. End quote. John Calvin is blunter about the cost. He says, quote, we must go after him through exiles, flights, reproaches, and all kinds of afflictions. End quote. That is not a comfortable sentence. But notice the structure of the verse. We are not going out into nowhere. We are going out to him. The outside is where he is. If you ever feel that following Jesus has cost you a seat at a table, this verse says you have not been abandoned. You have been moved to where he is standing.

Verse fourteen gives the reason. For here we have no lasting city, but we seek the city that is to come. This ties back to chapter eleven, where Abraham looked for a city with foundations. The whole letter has been saying that this world is not the final home. If that is true, then losing your standing in it is not the worst thing.

Verse fifteen turns to what we do while we wait. Through him then let us continually offer up a sacrifice of praise to God. Calvin comments that praise, quote, is the highest worship of God, justly preferred to all other exercises. End quote. Verse sixteen adds, do not neglect to do good and to share what you have, for such sacrifices are pleasing to God. So the sacrifices have not disappeared. They have changed. The lips give thanks and the hands give help.

Verse seventeen is about leaders. Obey your leaders and submit to them, for they are keeping watch over your souls, as those who will have to give an account. Let them do this with joy and not with groaning, for that would be of no advantage to you. Chrysostom has a sharp observation here. He says, quote, Let those who rule also hear, and not only those who are under their rule. End quote. He goes on to say that, quote, as the subjects ought to be obedient, so also the rulers ought to be watchful and sober. End quote. The verse is a charge in both directions. Pastors will give an account, and that should keep them sober. Members should make the pastor's work a joy, and not a groan.

Then the writer gets personal. Pray for us, he says, for we are sure that we have a clear conscience. He wants to be restored to them soon. And in verses twenty and twenty-one comes one of the most beautiful prayers in the New Testament. Now may the God of peace who brought again from the dead our Lord Jesus, the great shepherd of the sheep, by the blood of the eternal covenant, equip you with everything good that you may do his will, working in us that which is pleasing in his sight, through Jesus Christ, to whom be glory forever and ever. Amen.

Look at what the prayer asks. It does not ask us to try harder. It asks God to equip us and to work in us what pleases him. The same God who raised Jesus from the dead is the one who does the work inside you. That is the answer to the question of how a person can possibly go outside the camp.

Then a short, gracious ending. I appeal to you, brothers, bear with my word of exhortation, for I have written to you briefly. The writer calls this long and weighty letter brief. He mentions that Timothy has been released, and he sends greetings from those who come from Italy. And the last words are the whole letter in a breath. Grace be with all of you.

Let me draw a few threads together.

Look at what Luke and Hebrews share. Both end with a blessing. Luke has Jesus lifting his hands. Hebrews has the writer lifting a prayer, asking the God of peace to equip his people. Both endings say that the Christian life begins and ends in something given. The disciples wait to be clothed with power. The readers are equipped by God. Proverbs agrees, in its way, because the woman in the poem has nothing that the fear of the Lord did not give her.

Here is a question to carry through your day. Where in your life are you trying to produce something that you are supposed to receive? Wisdom, strength, patience, peace. Ask for it, and wait if you have to.
