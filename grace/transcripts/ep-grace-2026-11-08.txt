Zeal for your house will consume me.

That is what the disciples remembered when they watched Jesus come into the temple with a whip in his hand. And I want you to hold that line next to another one, from Ecclesiastes today. Guard your steps when you go to the house of God. Two very different men, standing at the same door. One of them walks in slowly and quietly, and the other one walks in and turns over the tables.

Good morning, Grace, and welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day seven of month ten, and today we have three readings. Ecclesiastes, chapter five. John, chapter two, verses twelve through twenty-five. And James, chapter three, verses thirteen through eighteen.

Yesterday we stood at a wedding in Cana and watched Jesus turn six stone jars of water into wine, and we said that his hour had not yet come. Today we see him in Jerusalem, and the hour begins to cast its shadow. Yesterday James told us that nobody can tame the tongue, and that blessing and cursing should not come out of the same mouth. Today he gives us the cure, a wisdom that comes from above. And the Preacher, who spent yesterday among the lonely and the oppressed, now takes us to church, and the first thing he talks about is our mouths. So you can see the thread. Words, wisdom, and the house of God. Let us begin with the Preacher.

Ecclesiastes, chapter five.

This chapter is built like a walk with a stop at four places. It begins in the house of God in verses one through seven. It moves into the world of power and money in verses eight through twelve. It looks at wealth that is lost in verses thirteen through seventeen. And it ends with a quiet answer in verses eighteen through twenty. Worship, injustice, money, and gift.

Start with worship. Verse one. Guard your steps when you go to the house of God. To draw near to listen is better than to offer the sacrifice of fools, for they do not know that they are doing evil.

Notice the contrast. Listening is better than sacrificing. That does not mean sacrifice is wrong, since God commanded it. It means that a person can perform the whole ritual and never once hear anything. The fool in Proverbs and Ecclesiastes is not stupid. He is careless. He comes to God the way a man walks through a train station, quickly, with his mind on the next thing.

Matthew Henry, commenting on these verses, puts the problem in a single line. Quote. Many words and hasty ones, used in prayer, show folly in the heart. End quote. And the Pulpit Commentary, on the next verse, says it just as plainly. Quote. The warning is against hasty and thoughtless words in prayer. End quote.

Verse two gives the reason. Be not rash with your mouth, and let not your heart be hasty to utter a word before God, for God is in heaven and you are on earth. Therefore let your words be few. John Gill draws out the picture of a king. Quote. Men should consider before they speak to the King of kings. End quote. Think about how carefully you would choose your words if you were summoned before a head of state. This is a far greater court, and it is open every morning.

Now, please hear what the Preacher is not saying. He is not telling you to pray less. He is telling you to hurry less. In the Psalms, people pour out their hearts to God in loud words, and the same Bible tells them to be still. The common thread is that God is God. Verse three adds a proverb, that a dream comes with much business and a fool's voice with many words. Busy mind, busy mouth. Both are noise.

Verses four through six move to vows. When you vow a vow to God, do not delay paying it. It is better that you should not vow than that you should vow and not pay. That echoes Deuteronomy twenty-three, where Moses says much the same thing. A vow is a promise spoken out loud with God listening. The Preacher does not forbid it. He only asks you to mean it. And notice the odd phrase in verse six, do not say before the messenger that it was a mistake. The messenger is probably the priest or temple officer who came to collect on the promise. The temptation is to say, I did not really mean it. And the Preacher says, do not do that to God. Verse seven closes the section with a little summary, that much dreaming and many words are vanity, but, and here is the main word, fear God.

Then, without any warning, the scene changes. Verse eight. If you see in a province the oppression of the poor and the violation of justice and righteousness, do not be amazed at the matter, for the high official is watched by a higher, and there are higher ones over them.

This is a hard verse, and the commentators are divided over it. One reading takes it as cynical, that there is a ladder of bureaucrats each watching the one below, and nobody ever brings justice. Another reading takes it as hopeful, that above the highest official stands the highest of all, and that is God. The Preacher is probably speaking out of both. He is telling you not to be shocked that injustice runs through the system, because every system has layers. And he is also reminding you that a higher One is watching, which fits everything he has just said about the God who is in heaven. Yesterday we heard that the oppressed had no one to comfort them. Today he says, someone is watching, even when it does not feel like it.

Now money. Verse ten. He who loves money will not be satisfied with money, nor he who loves wealth with his income. This also is vanity.

Matthew Poole says it simply. Quote. The greatest treasures of silver do not satisfy the covetous possessor of it. End quote. And the Cambridge Bible calls it, quote, the conspicuous fact. End quote. That is, you do not have to search long to see it. Everyone who has money wants more. The Preacher lists the reasons. When goods increase, those who eat them increase. The owner sees them only with his eyes. A laborer sleeps soundly, whether he eats little or much, but the full stomach of the rich will not let him sleep.

Then comes one of the most sobering images in the book. Verse fifteen. As he came from his mother's womb he shall go again, naked as he came, and shall take nothing for his toil that he may carry away in his hand. No hearse has a luggage rack, and you know that.

But the Preacher does not end in gloom. Verse eighteen. Behold, what I have seen to be good and fitting is to eat and drink and find enjoyment in all the toil with which one toils under the sun the few days of his life that God has given him, for this is his lot. And then verse nineteen says that when God gives a person wealth and possessions, and power to enjoy them, this is the gift of God. The key word there is gift. In verse twenty, the Preacher says such a person will not much remember the days of his life, because God keeps him occupied with joy in his heart.

So here is the practical shape of the chapter. When you come near to God, listen more than you speak. When you see injustice, do not be shocked, and remember who sits above the highest official. When you have money, hold it loosely, because you will leave it. And whatever you do have, today's meal, today's work, today's family, receive it as a gift. Not a project, not a prize, but a gift. That is a way to sit in a pew and a way to sit at a dinner table.

John, chapter two, verses twelve through twenty-five.

Yesterday ended at Cana. Verse twelve is a quiet transition. After this he went down to Capernaum, with his mother and his brothers and his disciples, and they stayed there for a few days. Then, verse thirteen. The Passover of the Jews was at hand, and Jesus went up to Jerusalem.

Notice the verb, went up. Jerusalem sits on a ridge, and in Scripture one always goes up to it. This is the first of three Passovers that John records in his Gospel. That matters for the whole book, because John structures his story around these feasts, and each one brings Jesus closer to the cross. The Passover was the great feast of deliverance, the night the lamb's blood was on the doorposts. And Jesus walks into Jerusalem at exactly that season.

He finds in the temple those who were selling oxen and sheep and pigeons, and the money-changers sitting there. Now, this was not a street market that had wandered into the temple by accident. The pilgrims needed animals without blemish, and they needed the right coins to pay the temple tax, so the traders set up in the outer court, the court of the Gentiles. It was convenient. It was even useful. And the place where people from every nation were meant to pray had become a market with a smell and a noise.

Jesus makes a whip of cords, drives them all out of the temple with the sheep and oxen, pours out the coins of the money-changers, and overturns their tables. To those who sold the pigeons, the poorest man's offering, he says, take these things away. Do not make my Father's house a house of trade.

Look at that phrase, my Father's house. Jesus has just called the temple his Father's, and no one in the court misses it.

Now, was he violent? Many readers have wondered. Albert Barnes answers calmly. Quote. There is no evidence that he used any violence to the men engaged in that unhallowed traffic. End quote. And the Pulpit Commentary says of the whip, quote, The scourge, as Godet says, is a symbol, not an instrument. End quote. Chrysostom, in his twenty-third homily on John, notices that Jesus did not rely on words alone. Quote. For He was not even satisfied with words only, but took a scourge, and so cast them out. End quote. Augustine, in his tenth tractate, sees the same whip as a sermon in itself. He says Jesus made a scourge of small cords, and with it, quote, lashed the unruly, who were making merchandise of God's temple. End quote. Whatever the details, the point is not hard to see. Jesus has authority here, the kind that makes a crowd of merchants step back for one man.

Verse seventeen is the hinge. His disciples remembered that it was written, Zeal for your house will consume me. That is Psalm sixty-nine, verse nine, a psalm of a righteous sufferer. The disciples did not understand it at the time, but later they saw it as a prophecy. Zeal means a burning, protective love, and the psalm says it will consume him. It will use him up. That is the shadow I mentioned. The same zeal that clears the temple will lead to the cross. Spurgeon preached a sermon called The Zeal of Christ on this text, and he said Christ, quote, could not rest and be quiet. End quote. Zeal is not temper. It is love that cannot stand still when something beloved is being dishonored.

Now the Jews respond, verse eighteen. What sign do you show us for doing these things? They are not asking whether he is right. They are asking who gave him the right.

And Jesus gives the most puzzling answer in the chapter. Destroy this temple, and in three days I will raise it up.

They think he means the building. The Jews said, it has taken forty-six years to build this temple, and will you raise it up in three days? That would be Herod's rebuilding, which started around 20 B.C. and was still going on. But John steps in with a comment in verse twenty-one. He was speaking about the temple of his body. And here is a small point of Greek that helps. John uses the word hieron for the temple complex in the earlier verses, the whole precinct with its courts and its stalls. But in verse nineteen the word is naos, the inner sanctuary, the place where God dwells. The Cambridge Bible notes that the term means, quote, the central sacred building. End quote. Jesus is saying, the place where God dwells is about to be standing in front of you.

Gill pictures Jesus as, quote, pointing, as it were, with his finger to his body. End quote. We cannot be sure he did, but it fits. Augustine settles the interpretation in one sentence. Quote. But He spoke, says the evangelist, of the temple of His body. End quote. And R. C. Sproul, in his commentary on John, puts the claim in its simplest form. Quote. Christ is the temple, and all men are commanded to come to Him in order to worship and serve the one true God. End quote.

So here is the hardest question in the passage. Why did Jesus say it so cryptically, and why did he say, destroy? Think about the grammar. It is a command, but it is not a request. Poole and others read it as a prediction, as if to say, this is what you are going to do. The leaders will indeed destroy the temple of his body, and he will raise it. The disciples did not understand it until afterward. Verse twenty-two says that when he was raised from the dead, his disciples remembered that he had said this, and they believed the Scripture and the word that Jesus had spoken. That is a good pattern for Bible reading. Often you read something and do not see it until later, and the later is when the light comes on.

Now the passage ends with a sentence that every honest reader should stop at. While he was in Jerusalem at the Passover feast, many believed in his name when they saw the signs that he was doing. But Jesus on his part did not entrust himself to them, because he knew all people.

There is a wordplay in the Greek. The word believed and the word entrust are the same word. Many believed in him, but he did not believe in them. It is a quiet and rather chilling line. The Pulpit Commentary captures it in a short sentence. Quote. He had no faith in their faith. End quote. John Gill says that Jesus did not trust himself with them, quote, on the basis of his miracles. End quote. Their faith rested on spectacle. Matthew Henry says plainly that, quote, carnal, empty professors are not to be trusted. End quote. This is not a harsh verdict on all of the crowd, since some of them would become true disciples. It is an observation that interest in Jesus is not the same as trust in Jesus. And the next chapter starts with a man who came to Jesus by night, who is a bit of both.

So the application. Two questions from this chapter. First, what has become a marketplace in the place where you meet God? Not in a building, but in the quiet hour, in the Sunday morning, in your own mind. What crowds out the prayer? Second, are you the kind of believer Jesus can trust himself to? He is not asking for a perfect faith. He is asking for a faith that stays when the signs get quiet. And the good news of this chapter is that the one who is burning with zeal for his Father's house is the same one who will, for your sake, be consumed.

James, chapter three, verses thirteen through eighteen.

Yesterday James left us with a problem. The tongue is untamable, and the same mouth blesses and curses. And now he asks a question that changes the subject. Who is wise and understanding among you?

I think James does that on purpose. He has been talking about speech, and now he moves to the source of speech. What comes out of the mouth is only the surface. Behind it is a kind of wisdom, and there are two kinds.

Verse thirteen. Let him show it by his good conduct, with deeds done in the meekness of wisdom. John Gill reads wisdom here as, quote, not in things natural and civil, or merely moral, but in things spiritual. End quote. And Albert Barnes states the point briefly. Quote. True wisdom is always meek, mild, gentle. End quote. Notice what is missing. James does not say that the wise are the ones with the best arguments, or the most books. The wise show it in good conduct, and in meekness. In the Bible, wisdom is not mostly about how much you know. It is about how you live.

Now the dark side. Verse fourteen. But if you have bitter jealousy and selfish ambition in your heart, do not boast and be false to the truth. The Cambridge Bible explains the second word, the one translated selfish ambition, as, quote, faction and party-spirit of any kind. End quote. It is the spirit that treats every conversation as a contest. And Matthew Poole says of the bitter zeal that James mentions, that it, quote, is really no other than envy. End quote. That is quite a statement. The man may call his zeal for the truth, but James says it is envy in disguise.

I want you to notice something here. We just read about Jesus' zeal, in John. And now James warns about a different zeal, the bitter kind. How do you tell them apart? The zeal of Jesus was for his Father's house, and cost him everything. The bitter zeal James warns about is for my own reputation, and costs other people. One consumes itself for God. The other consumes everyone else for itself.

Verse fifteen. This is not the wisdom that comes down from above, but is earthly, unspiritual, demonic. Three steps downward. Earthly, which belongs to this world only. Unspiritual, which is the word for natural, running without the Spirit. And demonic, which is a way to describe wisdom that works like the devil. Verse sixteen gives the result. For where jealousy and selfish ambition exist, there will be disorder and every vile practice. Where there is rivalry, there is chaos. You can see it in churches, in families, and in offices.

And then the great turn in verse seventeen. But the wisdom from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, open to reason, full of mercy and good fruits, impartial and sincere.

That is a list of seven qualities, and they are worth taking slowly. Pure. Peaceable. Gentle. Open to reason, which is a rare Greek word, meaning easy to persuade, willing to yield. Full of mercy and good fruits. Impartial, which takes us back to chapter two and the gold ring. And sincere, with no mask.

Matthew Poole asks why purity comes first. His answer is helpful. Quote. Peaceableness, which relates to man, is set after purity, which respects God in the first place. End quote. That is, you cannot make peace at the cost of holiness. The Cambridge Bible adds a caution, that, quote, the sequence is that of thought, not of time. End quote. James is not saying that you first become pure for ten years and then start to be peaceful. He is saying that purity is the root, and peace is what grows up from it. And Barnes reminds us what kind of list this is. Quote. This does not refer to the doctrines of religion, but to its spirit. End quote.

I find that useful. Many of us think we need more correct doctrine, and we may. But James points out that a person with all the right doctrine and a bitter spirit is a wisdom that has not come from above. The wisdom that comes from above looks like Jesus. It is pure. It is peaceable. It is gentle.

And verse eighteen is the promise. And a harvest of righteousness is sown in peace by those who make peace. Notice the picture is farming. Peace is a seed, and righteousness is the harvest. You do not get righteousness by fighting for it. You get it by sowing peace.

And that is the answer to yesterday. We cannot tame the tongue by willpower. But God gives wisdom to anyone who asks, as James said in chapter one, and the tongue follows the heart.

Closing.

Let me draw out three threads, and only because I think they are real. In Ecclesiastes, the Preacher says, guard your steps, let your words be few. In James, a bitter tongue grows from a bitter heart, and a wise heart grows a gentle tongue. And in John, Jesus cleared the temple not out of anger for himself, but out of love for his Father. In each case, what matters is not the display, but what is under it. The Preacher says the fool offers sacrifices without listening. James says the envious person claims wisdom without meekness. And John says the crowd believed Jesus' signs without trusting him. They all ask the same quiet question. What is really going on in you?

So here is your question for the day. When you speak today, what is under the words? And when you come to God, will you listen first?
