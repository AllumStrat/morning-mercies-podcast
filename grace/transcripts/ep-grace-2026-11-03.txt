What are you looking for?

It is the first thing Jesus says in the Gospel of John. Not a sermon, not a miracle, not a title. Just a question, asked of two men who are walking behind him and have not yet said a word. What are you looking for? Hold that question. We will come back to it.

Good morning, Grace, and welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day four of month ten, and today we have three readings. In Ecclesiastes, chapter three, verses one through fifteen. In John, chapter one, verses thirty-five through forty-two. And in James, chapter two, verses one through thirteen.

Yesterday John the Baptist finally named Jesus as the Lamb of God, and we ended on the old guilt that comes back at night. Today the Baptist says it a second time, and this time somebody is listening closely enough to get up and follow. His pointing bears fruit. And in James, we left off with pure religion, which turned out to be a bridled tongue and care for orphans and widows. Today James shows us what that looks like inside a church service, and it is not comfortable. But first, the Preacher, who yesterday told us that enjoyment comes from the hand of God. Today he tells us why that hand is not hurried.

Ecclesiastes three, verses one through fifteen.

This is one of the best known passages in the whole Bible, and one of the most misread. It opens with a line most of us can say from memory. For everything there is a season, and a time for every matter under heaven. Then comes a poem. A time to be born, and a time to die. A time to plant, and a time to pluck up what is planted. A time to weep, and a time to laugh. A time to mourn, and a time to dance. Fourteen pairs, twenty-eight times, and the word time appears in every line.

Notice two different words in the opening line. The word season is the Hebrew zeman. The Pulpit Commentary says it denotes, quote, a fixed, definite portion of time. End quote. And Albert Barnes adds that this rare word means emphatically, quote, fitting time. End quote. The second word, translated time, is eth, which is more the moment when something happens. So the Preacher is saying two things at once. There is a stretch of time that God has set for each thing, and there is a right moment within it. Not every day is for every thing. There is a day to tear down and a day to build, and wisdom is knowing which day you are standing in.

And that is exactly the point. Remember where the king has been these last two days. He has been reaching, building, hoarding, hating the fact that he cannot control what comes after him. Now he steps back and says, in effect, you are not the one holding the clock. Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, puts it plainly. Quote. To expect unchanging happiness in a changing world, must end in disappointment. End quote. Much of our weariness comes from demanding that one season last, or that another hurry up.

Then verse nine asks the question that has been hanging over the book since chapter one. What gain has the worker from his toil? And verse ten says he has seen the business that God has given to the children of man to be busy with. Then comes verse eleven, which is the center of the whole passage. He has made everything beautiful in its time. Also, he has put eternity into man's heart, yet so that man cannot find out what God has done from the beginning to the end.

Read that again slowly, because it holds two claims that pull against each other. First, God makes things beautiful in their time. The Pulpit Commentary says, quote, all parts of this have, in God's design, a beauty and a harmony, their own season for appearance and development. End quote. Even the funeral. Even the pulling up of what was planted. Not beautiful to us in the moment, but beautiful in his design, in its time.

Second, he has put eternity in the human heart. The Hebrew word is olam, which means a long duration, a time without a visible edge. What does it mean? Here the commentators disagree, and I want you to hear that. Albert Barnes reads it as a capacity. He says, quote, God has placed in the inborn constitution of man the capability of conceiving of eternity. End quote. The Cambridge Bible is more careful. It says, quote, the thought expressed is not that of the hope of an immortality, but rather the sense of the Infinite which precedes it. End quote. So the Cambridge Bible is saying that the Preacher is not yet arguing for life after death. He is describing an ache. We are creatures who live inside moments, and we cannot stop sensing that there is something bigger than the moment.

That ache is the problem and the gift at the same time. Because we are made to want the whole picture, and we are given only the part we are standing in. We look at a funeral and sense that this cannot be the whole story, and yet we cannot see the end from the beginning. The Preacher does not solve that tension. He names it honestly.

So what do we do with it? Verses twelve and thirteen answer, and you will recognize the refrain from yesterday. There is nothing better for them than to be joyful and to do good as long as they live. Also that everyone should eat and drink and take pleasure in all his toil, this is God's gift to man. Notice the two verbs. Be joyful, and do good. Enjoyment and generosity travel together. The Preacher does not tell us to wring our hands over what we cannot see. He tells us to do the next good thing, with gladness, inside the season we have.

Verse fourteen gives the foundation. I know that whatever God does endures forever. Nothing can be added to it, nor anything taken from it. God has done it, so that people fear before him. The reason you can rest inside a season you do not control is that the one who controls it does not make mistakes. Matthew Poole, commenting on verse eleven, says simply that it, quote, could not have been done better. End quote. If the clock in your life is in his hand, then the hour that feels wrong to you has not slipped past him.

John one, verses thirty-five through forty-two.

He said that yesterday, to the crowd. It did nothing. Today he says it to two men, and it changes their lives. The same words, a different hearing. That is worth sitting with. A sentence can pass over a crowd and land on two people.

Now, the scene. The Baptist is at the Jordan with two of his disciples, on a third day in John's counting. Watch what the two men do. The text says the two disciples heard him say this, and they followed Jesus. Albert Barnes sees something admirable in the Baptist here. He says, quote, this shows that John was sincere; that he was not desirous of forming a party or of building up a sect. End quote. Think about what that cost. John had spent months building a following. And the moment the right person walks by, John points away from himself, and two of his best students leave. Last time we heard him say, I am not the Christ. Today we watch what it looks like to mean it.

And here is the part of the scene I find most moving. The Pulpit Commentary notes, quote, the lonely Christ has as yet not called one disciple into his fellowship. End quote. Jesus has been baptized, tempted, and has walked back to the river, and so far nobody has joined him. No one is on his team. Then two men start walking behind him, not even speaking, just following at a distance. And in verse thirty-eight, Jesus turns around.

Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, makes an observation I want you to catch. Quote. Whatever communion there is between our souls and Christ, it is he who begins the discourse. End quote. They were following, but he is the one who speaks first. That is the pattern all through John's Gospel, and all through our lives. We think we found him. In fact, he turned around.

And his first words, as I said, are a question. What are you seeking? What do you want? The Greek verb is zeteo, to seek, to look for, to hunt. It is the same verb John will use at the empty tomb, when Jesus asks Mary Magdalene, whom are you seeking? The Gospel begins and ends with Jesus asking what you are really after.

And look at their answer. It is a little awkward. They do not say, we are looking for the Messiah. They say, Rabbi, where are you staying? Teacher, where do you live? It is what people say when they want more time and do not know how to ask for it. John adds a note for his Greek readers, that Rabbi means Teacher.

Here is a key word. Staying. The Greek is meno, which means to remain, to abide, to dwell. It is a favorite word of John. It appears in this one little passage three times, where are you staying, they saw where he was staying, and they stayed with him that day. By the time we reach the upper room, Jesus will say, abide in me, and I in you. It begins here, with two uncertain men asking where he lives.

And Jesus' answer is one of the most generous in the Gospels. Come and you will see. He does not give directions. He does not give an address. He does not give a lecture. He gives an invitation. Albert Barnes calls it, quote, a kind and gracious answer. End quote. There is a lot of work done in this passage by the word see. Come and you will see. It will happen again in verse forty-six, when Nathanael doubts and Philip says the very same thing. Come and see. In John, you do not get to know Jesus by argument first. You get to know him by being in the room.

But what hour was it? Here is a real disagreement, and it is interesting. Matthew Poole says the tenth hour is, quote, about four of the clock afternoon, which answers the tenth hour according to the Jewish account. End quote. That counts from sunrise, so ten hours after about six is four o'clock. But John Gill, the Baptist, takes it the other way. He says that on the Roman way of reckoning, it, quote, must be ten o'clock in the morning. End quote. If Gill is right, they had the whole day with Jesus. If Poole is right, they had the evening. Either way, the text says they stayed that day. And Gill writes that they spent the rest of the day, quote, in delightful conversation with him. End quote. I like Gill's phrase. John does not tell us a word of what was said. He only tells us that it was long enough that Andrew came out sure he had found the Messiah.

Verse thirty-nine adds a detail. It was about the tenth hour. John remembers the very time of day. Poole counts that as four in the afternoon, and Gill says the day was spent, quote, in delightful conversation with him. End quote. John does not tell us a word of what was said.

Because that is what Andrew says next. Verse forty introduces one of the two. He is Andrew, Simon Peter's brother. The other is not named. Most think the unnamed one is John himself, the author, who in this Gospel never names himself. Notice what Andrew does the moment he leaves. He finds his own brother Simon first. Not a stranger. Not a crowd. His brother. And he says, we have found the Messiah. The word is the Hebrew mashiach, anointed one, and John translates it for us, Christ. After a single afternoon, Andrew uses the biggest word in Israel's hope.

John Calvin has a line on Andrew that I find wonderfully humane. Quote. Andrew has scarcely a spark, and yet, by means of it, he enlightens his brother. End quote. Scarcely a spark. Andrew is not a theologian yet. He does not understand the cross. He has no idea what is coming. He has spent one day with Jesus, and he knows two things, that Jesus is the one, and that his brother needs to hear. And that small spark lights Simon Peter, the man who will preach at Pentecost.

So what is the hardest question here? I would say it is this. Why does Jesus ask them what they are seeking, when he already knows? He is God. He knew. The answer, I think, is that he is not asking for information. He is asking so that they will say it out loud, so that they will have to find out what they actually came for. And they answer honestly. They want to be where he is.

Jesus renames Simon on sight. Calvin says Christ, quote, promises what Peter had not at all expected, and thus magnifies his own grace to all ages. End quote. Peter did not earn the name Cephas, which is Aramaic for rock. It was spoken over him before he could do a single thing.

Which brings us to the application, and I want to keep it small, the way the text does. Ask yourself the question as if Jesus had turned around and asked it of you this morning. What are you looking for? Not what you should say. What you are really looking for. Security? Approval? Rest? A way out? The beautiful thing is that the right answer is not required. The two men gave an awkward answer, and the reply was an invitation. Come, and you will see. And then, like Andrew, you do not need to understand much to tell one person. Name one person this week, a brother, a neighbor, a friend, who needs to hear that you spent time with him and it was worth it. You do not need a speech. A spark is enough.

James two, verses one through thirteen.

Now James. If you have been reading along with us, you know this letter is very direct. James is writing to Jewish Christians scattered abroad, to people who are poor, and sometimes persecuted, and now, we find, sometimes showing favoritism. Chapter one ended with true religion, caring for orphans and widows, and keeping yourself unstained from the world. Now James gets concrete. Here is what unstained looks like in the Sunday gathering.

Verse one is the thesis. My brothers, show no partiality as you hold the faith in our Lord Jesus Christ, the Lord of glory. The word for partiality is prosopolempsia, which literally means to receive the face. To take a person according to how they look. And he puts it right next to the glory of Christ. It is as if James says, if you really believed that Jesus is the Lord of glory, you would stop being impressed by lesser glory.

The Cambridge Bible for Schools makes a point I had not thought of. It says that Christ, quote, had shewn Himself, through His whole life on earth, to be no respecter of persons. End quote. Think of it. The Lord of glory ate with tax collectors, spoke to a Samaritan woman, touched lepers, and called fishermen. Nobody who followed him can use the world's ranking and call it Christian.

Then James paints a scene, and it is almost comic if it were not so sad. Verse two. If a man wearing a gold ring and fine clothing comes into your assembly, and a poor man in shabby clothing also comes in, and you pay attention to the one who wears the fine clothing and say, you sit here in a good place, while you say to the poor man, you stand over there, or sit down at my feet. The word for assembly is synagoge, which tells us how early and how Jewish this letter is. The usher making the split-second decision is us.

Verse four asks the sharp question. Have you not then made distinctions among yourselves and become judges with evil thoughts? Notice what James calls it. Not rudeness. Not bad manners. He calls it judging, and he says the thoughts behind it are evil. You have taken a seat that belongs to God.

Then comes the argument, and it comes in three moves. The first is in verse five. Listen, my beloved brothers, has not God chosen those who are poor in the world to be rich in faith and heirs of the kingdom, which he has promised to those who love him? Albert Barnes explains it carefully. He says, quote, though poor in this world's goods, they are rich in a higher and more important sense. End quote. James is not saying that every poor person is saved, or that wealth is wicked. He is saying that God's choosing has not followed the world's ranking. When you honor the rich man and insult the poor man, you may be insulting someone God has already honored.

John Gill adds a phrase that I think is lovely. On the opening verse he says that, quote, the poor as well as the rich are espoused by him, as their Lord and husband. End quote. Espoused. Betrothed. The poor man in shabby clothing at the back of your church is engaged to the Lord of glory.

The third move is the heart, and this is where James quotes the Law. Verse eight. If you really fulfill the royal law according to the Scripture, you shall love your neighbor as yourself, you are doing well. But if you show partiality, you are committing sin and are convicted by the law as transgressors. He calls it the royal law, because it comes from the King, and because it is the law of the kingdom. It comes from Leviticus nineteen, verse eighteen.

Then comes the hard part. Verse ten. For whoever keeps the whole law but fails in one point has become accountable for all of it. People find that verse difficult. Is James saying that every sin is equally bad? No. He is saying that the law is one thing, not a menu. The same God who said do not commit adultery also said do not murder, and if you break any command, you have rejected the lawgiver. You cannot say, I kept most of it. You cannot say, I loved the poor man in my heart, I just did not give him a seat. The law does not ask for your average. It asks for you.

So where does that leave us? Verse twelve. So speak and so act as those who are to be judged under the law of liberty. Law of liberty. We heard those words yesterday in chapter one. The gospel does not abolish the law. It sets us free to keep it. And look at what James holds in front of us. We will be judged. Not so that we will despair, but so that we will live like people who know it.

And then verse thirteen, which is the one the whole paragraph has been walking toward. For judgment is without mercy to one who has shown no mercy. Mercy triumphs over judgment.

Matthew Poole explains that such a person, quote, shall be judged according to the rigour of the law, by pure justice without any mixture of mercy. End quote. Matthew Henry says the same, that the doom of the impenitent will be, quote, judgment without mercy. End quote. This is not a threat that you must earn salvation by being merciful. R. C. Sproul, quoted in Tabletalk, the Ligonier magazine, puts the point from the other side, that by God's law, quote, we are commanded to temper justice with mercy. End quote. A heart that has truly received mercy cannot stay hard. Mercy shown is the evidence, not the price.

And then the last half. Mercy triumphs over judgment. The Cambridge Bible says, quote, it is true of man's judgment, but also of God's, that mercy triumphs over severity, when it finds a willing object. End quote. A willing object. Someone who will take it. The Pulpit Commentary notes that the sentence comes, as it says, almost like a cry of triumph. That is exactly how it reads. James has spent twelve verses walking through law, and judgment, and failure, and then he lets out a shout. Mercy wins.

This is what the church is for. A place where the one with the gold ring and the one with the stained coat are both welcomed, because both came the same way, as people who needed mercy and found it.

Here is one. Ecclesiastes says God has made everything beautiful in its time. John tells us, with an almost surprising precision, that it was about the tenth hour. Two men walking behind a stranger on a late afternoon looked like an ordinary event. It was an appointed moment, in a season God had set. If you ever feel that your life is happening at the wrong time, remember that John could still tell you the hour.

And here is another. In all three readings, the people with no standing are the ones God chooses. Two unnamed disciples. A fisherman called a rock before he had done anything. A poor man at the back of the assembly who is, in God's sight, rich in faith. God goes looking for what has been driven away.

So here is the question to carry through the day. Jesus turns and asks, what are you looking for? And then, when someone comes into the room today, someone who does not look like they belong, what will you see first, the gold ring, or the person Christ has already named?
