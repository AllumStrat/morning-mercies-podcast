They have no wine.

Four words, spoken quietly at a wedding in a small Galilean village, and they set in motion the first sign of Jesus's public ministry. Hold that thought, because later today James is going to talk about the words that come out of our mouths, and Mary's four words are about as good a use of the tongue as you will find anywhere.

Good morning, Grace, and welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day six of month ten, and today we have three readings. Ecclesiastes, chapter four. John, chapter two, verses one through eleven. And James, chapter three, verses one through twelve.

Yesterday Jesus saw Nathanael under the fig tree, and Nathanael, you may remember, was from Cana. Today we find out what happens in Cana. Yesterday James told us that words without deeds, a polite go in peace to a hungry neighbor, are worth nothing. Today he turns to the mouth itself. And yesterday the Preacher looked at a crooked courtroom and said that God will judge. Today he keeps looking, and what he sees is more crushing still. So let us start where the Preacher starts, with the tears.

Ecclesiastes, chapter four.

Verse one. Again I saw all the oppressions that are done under the sun. And behold, the tears of the oppressed, and they had no one to comfort them. On the side of their oppressors there was power, and there was no one to comfort them.

Notice the sentence that is repeated, no one to comfort them. It appears twice in a single verse. Last time, the Preacher watched injustice from a distance, in the courts. Now he is standing among the people it falls on, and he can see their faces. The Pulpit Commentary says of that phrase, quote, there was no one to wipe away their tears. End quote. I think that is exactly the picture. Not a lack of speeches or of policy, but a lack of someone with a hand and a handkerchief.

And then the Preacher says something that startles us. Verse two. I thought the dead who are already dead more fortunate than the living who are still alive. But better than both is he who has not yet been, who has not seen the evil deeds that are done under the sun.

Now be careful. This is not the Bible telling you to wish you had never been born. The Preacher is a witness reporting what a man feels when he looks only at the weight of oppression and sees nothing beyond it. Matthew Henry reads it that way. If this world and the things of it were our chief good, then not existing would be better than living here. And that is his point. Take away God, and take away the world to come, and the honest conclusion is bleak. The Preacher is letting you feel that conclusion fully so that you will not settle for anything cheaper.

Then in verse four he turns from the victim to the striver. Then I saw that all toil and all skill in work come from a man's envy of his neighbor. This too is vanity and a striving after wind.

That is an uncomfortable sentence. A great deal of our hard work is not love of the work. It is rivalry. The Cambridge Bible notes the cost of success in these cases, that, quote, they find it has the drawback of drawing on them the envy and ill-will of their less successful neighbours. End quote. You work harder to be seen to win, and the winning makes someone else resentful, and the resentment feeds the next round. Everybody is running and nobody is arriving.

Verse five gives the opposite ditch. The fool folds his hands and eats his own flesh. That is laziness, and it destroys the man. So the Preacher does not recommend either one. Verse six is the balance. Better is a handful of quietness than two hands full of toil and a striving after wind. Matthew Henry puts the cost of the lazy one in a short line. Quote. Idleness is a sin that is its own punishment. End quote. The handful of quietness is the middle road. Work, yes, but stop letting envy hold the whip.

Then comes the lonely man of verses seven and eight. There was a man alone, without a second, having neither son nor brother, yet there was no end to all his toil, and his eyes were never satisfied with riches. And then the question that gives the man away. For whom am I toiling and depriving myself of pleasure? The Hebrew word for the second one there is sheni, a companion, someone beside you. He has no one. He has endless work and no one to hand it to, and notice that he never even asks the question until it is too late.

That sets up the best-loved verses in the chapter. Two are better than one, because they have a good reward for their toil. For if they fall, one will lift up his fellow. But woe to him who is alone when he falls and has not another to lift him up.

The Cambridge Bible pictures it on a mountain path. Quote. If one slip or stumble on a steep or rocky path the other is at hand to raise him. End quote. Then verse eleven adds warmth, two lying together keep warm, and verse twelve adds protection, though a man might prevail against one who is alone, two will withstand him. And then the famous line. A threefold cord is not quickly broken.

Many weddings use that verse, and it is lovely there, though the Preacher is speaking of friendship and partnership more widely. Matthew Henry carries it a step further, and reads the third strand as Christ. Quote. Christ will by his Spirit come to them; then there is a threefold cord. End quote. You do not have to press that into the verse to see what Henry means. A bond that includes God holds when a bond of two people alone sometimes does not.

The chapter ends with a little story in verses thirteen through sixteen. A poor and wise youth is better than an old and foolish king who no longer knows how to take advice. The youth rises from prison to the throne, and the crowds cheer him. And then the Preacher says that those who come after will not rejoice in him either. Popularity does not last. The king is replaced, and the replacement is forgotten. That too is a striving after wind.

So here is how I would put the chapter. The Preacher shows you three things that cannot be fixed from within the world. The tears of the oppressed, the envy that drives the ambitious, and the crowd that forgets its hero. And the one thing he offers that does hold is a person who will pick you up. Which is exactly where John is taking us next. In a village wedding, a family is about to be embarrassed, and someone notices.

John, chapter two, verses one through eleven.

On the third day there was a wedding at Cana in Galilee, and the mother of Jesus was there. Jesus also was invited to the wedding with his disciples.

The third day. John has been counting days since chapter one. Day one, the delegation questions the Baptist. Day two, the Lamb of God. Day three, two disciples follow Jesus. Day four, Philip and Nathanael. And now, the third day after that, a wedding. Some have noticed that this puts us at the end of a week. John's opening chapter was a new creation week, and it ends in a feast.

The setting matters. A village wedding in first century Galilee was not a one-evening affair. It could run for days, and the family were responsible for feeding the whole community. To run out of wine was not merely awkward. It was a social disgrace that a family could carry for years. Spurgeon, in his notes on this chapter, says of the couple, quote, they had not been long married before there was want in the house. End quote. A little humor there, and a lot of kindness.

Augustine, in his eighth tractate on John, says something I love about verse two. Quote. The Lord, on being invited, came to the marriage. End quote. Think about that. He did not come as an inspector. He came as a guest. The Son of God spent the first public act of his ministry at a party, with ordinary people, for ordinary joy.

Then comes Mary's report. They have no wine. And the reply that has troubled readers for two thousand years. Woman, what does this have to do with me? My hour has not yet come.

Let me take the hardest question first. Is Jesus being rude to his mother? Two things. First, in that culture, woman was a respectful form of address. He uses it again from the cross, when he provides for her. So the word itself is not cold. Second, the phrase, what to me and to you, is an idiom that means something like, what is there between us in this matter. It marks a boundary. It says, my path is not yours to direct.

John Gill takes the line as a statement about his mission. Quote. As God, he had a Father in heaven, whose business he came to do. End quote. Matthew Poole says the point is about timing, that Mary, quote, was not to prescribe the time to him when he should work miraculously. End quote. And Albert Barnes notes what follows, that, quote, it is clear that he did not intend to refuse to provide wine, but only to delay it a little. End quote. The Cambridge Bible says it most plainly. Quote. He will help, but in His own way, and in His own time. End quote.

John Calvin, in his commentary, says that Mary went beyond her proper place, even though she was not acting out of ambition. I will put it in my own words, not his, because I could verify only the sense of it. Calvin's point is that Jesus is not harsh, he is teaching her and us that his work is not on call to our schedule.

And what is the hour? In John, the hour is always the cross and the glory that comes through it. Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, ties the phrase to the other places in John where Jesus says his hour has not come, and says that Jesus works each thing at its proper season. He did the sign anyway, in a measure, as a foretaste. So the line is a hinge. A small hour at a wedding, pointing to the great hour at Calvary.

And look at Mary's response. She does not argue. She turns to the servants. Whatever he says to you, do it. It is the last thing Mary says in John's Gospel, and it is exactly the right thing to say. Spurgeon says of her, quote, this holy woman took the rebuke in silence. End quote. And then he adds how the servants responded, quote, they obeyed Christ to the letter. End quote. Mary's whole message is to point at him and tell them to do what he says.

Now the jars. John tells us there were six stone water jars there for the Jewish rites of purification, each holding twenty or thirty gallons. Jesus says, fill the jars with water, and they fill them to the brim. These were jars for ritual washing. They were meant to make people clean enough for God, over and over. They cannot do the thing they are for. And Jesus takes the water of that old, repeated cleansing and makes it wine. Six, in the Bible, is one short of seven, one short of completion. A hundred and twenty gallons at least of the best wine, enough to make the wedding legendary, and enough to say that the old system of washing was being fulfilled by something that will not run dry.

Then, draw some out and take it to the master of the feast. He tastes the water now become wine, not knowing where it came from, though the servants who had drawn the water knew. And the master calls the bridegroom and says, everyone serves the good wine first, and when people have drunk freely, then the poor wine. But you have kept the good wine until now.

The master of the feast is making a joke about weddings. But, as the Cambridge Bible notices, it is truer than he knows. Quote. The True Bridegroom was there, and had indeed kept the best dispensation until the last. End quote. In John's Gospel, the Baptist will say in the very next chapter, he who has the bride is the bridegroom. Jesus is quietly the one for whom the real wedding is arranged. And Spurgeon says it in one short line. Quote. He gives his people the best last. End quote.

Notice the pattern too. The world serves its best first and its worst later. Ecclesiastes has just shown that. The wine of ambition and applause is sweet at the start and sour at the end. Christ reverses it. Augustine says of the whole sign, in a phrase I find moving, quote, we were water, and He made us wine. End quote.

Then verse eleven. This, the first of his signs, Jesus did at Cana in Galilee, and manifested his glory. And his disciples believed in him. The Greek word for sign is semeion, and John uses it on purpose. A sign is not a stunt. It is a signpost. It points past itself. Gill says what the glory is, quote, the glory of his deity and divine sonship, which was hid by his assumption of human nature. End quote. The glory was there behind the flesh, and at Cana a corner of the curtain lifted.

Notice what Jesus did not do. He did not do it for the crowd. The master of the feast did not know. The guests did not know. The servants knew, and the disciples believed. The first glory of Christ in John was shown to the people who were doing the work, in the back, filling jars.

So what do we do with this? One application, and it is simple. When something in your life runs out, a plan, a patience, a joy, a bit of money, a family's peace, you can do what Mary did. You can say it to him in four words, they have no wine, and then stop. You do not tell him how to fix it, and you do not tell him when. You say it, and you do what he says next, even if what he says is to fill the jars with water, which looks like the wrong move. The Preacher said no one comforts the oppressed. John answers that at a wedding, Someone notices, and the party goes on.

James, chapter three, verses one through twelve.

James has been building toward this. In chapter one he said be quick to hear, slow to speak, and said that if anyone thinks he is religious and does not bridle his tongue, his religion is worthless. In chapter two, he said faith without works is dead, and used the example of a kindly word given in place of food. Now he stops and spends twelve verses on the single smallest organ most of us own.

Verse one. Not many of you should become teachers, my brothers, for you know that we who teach will be judged with greater strictness. Calvin takes this as a warning to people with a particular fault. He says the root problem is, quote, an innate disease in mankind to seek reputation by blaming others. End quote. That is James's point. The people who most want the chair at the front are often the ones who most enjoy correcting. And a teacher is judged more strictly because a teacher's words go into other people's lives. If you have ever been wounded by something someone said to you with authority, you know why.

Verse two. For we all stumble in many ways. And if anyone does not stumble in what he says, he is a perfect man, able also to bridle his whole body. Ellicott says of the verb, quote, to offend means to stumble over something, and fall. End quote. And notice the we. James includes himself. The brother of the Lord, the pillar of the Jerusalem church, says we all stumble. And Albert Barnes sees what he means by perfect, quote, a man always has complete government over himself if he has the entire control of his tongue. End quote. The tongue is the test case, because it is the hardest part of us to hold.

Then James gives three pictures. The bit in a horse's mouth, a small thing that turns a whole animal. The rudder on a ship, a small piece of wood that steers a ship driven by fierce winds. And the spark and the forest. How great a forest is set ablaze by such a small fire.

Calvin gets the logic, and says it in plain terms. Quote. Though the tongue be a small member, yet it avails much in regulating the life of man. End quote. Small does not mean harmless. The bit, the rudder, and the spark are all small, and all decisive. One sentence at the wrong time can change a marriage, a friendship, a congregation, a career.

And then verse six, which is as strong as anything in the New Testament. The tongue is a fire, a world of unrighteousness. The tongue is set among our members, staining the whole body, setting on fire the entire course of life, and set on fire by hell. The word for hell is Gehenna, the burning garbage valley outside Jerusalem. Calvin says, quote, the tongue pollutes the whole man. End quote. A fire is never a private matter. It spreads, and a rumor is the same.

Verses seven and eight take the thought one step further. Every kind of beast and bird, of reptile and sea creature, can be tamed and has been tamed by mankind, but no human being can tame the tongue. It is a restless evil, full of deadly poison. Matthew Henry puts it in a sentence worth underlining. Quote. No man can tame the tongue without Divine grace and assistance. End quote. That is the honest reading. James does not say you can't improve. He says you cannot do it alone.

R.C. Sproul, in his teaching on this chapter at Ligonier, begins from the same alarm. Quote. We all hold a very powerful weapon and are capable of doing violence to our neighbor. End quote. Notice the word violence. Not just rudeness. A weapon, and a weapon we carry everywhere.

Then verses nine and ten, the sharpest turn. With it we bless our Lord and Father, and with it we curse people who are made in the likeness of God. From the same mouth come blessing and cursing. My brothers, these things ought not to be so.

This is where I think James stops being general and starts being specific. The same tongue sings in church and sneers in the car. We are made to praise the Father in the morning and then tear down his image in the afternoon. And James is pointing at the image, made in the likeness of God. To curse a person is to curse a copy of God's own face. Calvin says it flatly. Quote. It is then a hypocrisy not to be borne, when man employs the same tongue in blessing God and in cursing men. End quote.

Finally, two pictures from nature. Does a spring pour forth from the same opening both fresh and salt water? Can a fig tree, my brothers, bear olives, or a grapevine produce figs? Neither can a salt pool yield fresh water. A tree is known by its fruit, and a spring by what flows. James's point is that a mouth that flows both ways is a sign of a heart that has not yet been made new. And this takes us right back to chapter two. Faith that has no works is dead, and a tongue that blesses God and curses people shows that the faith has not yet reached the mouth.

Now here is how this fits the whole letter. James has already told us where wisdom comes from. It comes from above, and God gives it generously. And at the end of chapter three, which we will read tomorrow, he describes the wisdom that comes down from heaven, pure, peaceable, gentle. The tongue chapter is the problem and tomorrow is the cure. We cannot tame the tongue ourselves. But God can give us wisdom, and wisdom shows up first in speech.

So where does that leave you? Not in despair, but in a prayer. Lord, set a guard over my mouth. And in a habit. Before you speak about someone, ask whether you would say it to their face, and whether you would say it with Christ standing in the room. Which, in light of Cana, he is.

Closing.

Let me draw out one connection, and only because I think it is real. In Ecclesiastes, the oppressed have no one to comfort them. In James, the tongue tears people down and cannot be tamed. And in John, Jesus walks into a room where a family is about to be shamed, and he turns water into wine, so that the shame never comes. Mary's four words, they have no wine, are the opposite of the tongue James describes. She did not gossip about the host, did not complain, did not accuse. She told the one who could help. And the one who could help noticed.

So here is your question for the day. When you open your mouth today, what will come out of it? Something that wipes a tear, or something that starts a fire? And when something runs dry in you, will you say it to Jesus first?

Tomorrow, on plan day seven, we are in John two, verses twelve through twenty-five, where Jesus goes to Jerusalem and cleans out the temple. In James three, verses thirteen through eighteen, the wisdom from above. And in Ecclesiastes five, where the Preacher tells us to guard our steps and our words when we go to the house of God.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
