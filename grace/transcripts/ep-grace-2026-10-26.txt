A woman walks to a grave before sunrise, carrying spices. She has thought about this for two days. She knows exactly what she will find, and she knows exactly what she will do about it. And a voice she does not recognize asks her a question she cannot answer. Why do you seek the living among the dead?

Good morning, Grace. This is Morning Mercies, and today is plan day nine twenty-one. We have three readings. Proverbs twenty-eight, verses fifteen through twenty-eight. Luke twenty-four, verses one through twelve. And Hebrews eleven, verses thirty-two through forty.

Last time, Luke left us at a sealed tomb with the women watching from a little way off. They saw the new grave and the fine linen, and they went home to prepare spices, and then they rested, because it was the Sabbath. Today the Sabbath is over. In Hebrews, the roll call of faith has been running for a whole chapter, and today it finally runs out of room. And in Proverbs, we pick up in the middle of Solomon's sayings about rulers, money, and the kind of person who can be trusted with either.

Let us begin with Proverbs.

Verses fifteen and sixteen are about power. Like a roaring lion or a charging bear is a wicked ruler over a poor people. A ruler who lacks understanding is a cruel oppressor, but he who hates unjust gain will prolong his days. Notice the two animals. A shepherd in the ancient world knew them as the two predators he had to drive off to protect the flock. David says as much to Saul about his own sheep. So the picture is a ruler who was supposed to be the shepherd and has become the thing the shepherd was there to fight. And notice the diagnosis in verse sixteen. The cruel ruler is a ruler who lacks understanding. Cruelty here is not strength. It is stupidity with an army behind it.

Verses seventeen and eighteen move to a man guilty of bloodshed, who flees to the pit, and the proverb says let no one help him. Then verse eighteen sets the two roads side by side. Whoever walks in integrity will be delivered, but he who is crooked in his ways will suddenly fall. That word suddenly is worth holding on to. Crooked living can look steady for years, and then it is over in an afternoon.

Verses nineteen through twenty-two are a little cluster about money. Verse nineteen is the same advice we heard earlier in the book. Work your land and you will have plenty of bread, and chase fantasies and you will have plenty of poverty. Verse twenty says the faithful man will abound with blessings, but whoever hastens to be rich will not go unpunished. The word is hurry. The proverb has nothing against wealth. It is against the shortcut. Verse twenty-one says that to show partiality is not good, and then adds a sharp little line, that a man will transgress for a piece of bread. Some people can be bought very cheap. Verse twenty-two describes the stingy man, literally the man with an evil eye, who hastens after wealth and does not know that poverty will come upon him.

Now the heart of the section, which begins at verse twenty-three. Whoever rebukes a man will afterward find more favor than he who flatters with his tongue. This is a promise with a delay built into it. Afterward. Nobody thanks you for the hard word on the day you say it. Matthew Henry says, "Upon reflection, most will have a better opinion of a faithful reprover than of a soothing flatterer." And John Gill, the Baptist commentator, puts it from the receiving side, that the man who was corrected "will love, value, and esteem his faithful friend and rebuker, more than the man that fawned upon him." Read that as a question about your own life, Grace. Who has permission to tell you what you do not want to hear? If the answer is nobody, then you are surrounded by flatterers, however kind they are.

Verse twenty-four is one of the hardest in the chapter. Whoever robs his father or his mother and says, it is no transgression, is a companion to a man who destroys. The excuse is the sin inside the sin. He has decided that taking from the people who gave him everything is not really stealing. Notice how often in this chapter wickedness has a story it tells itself.

Verse twenty-five says the greedy stirs up strife, but the one who trusts in the Lord will be enriched. And verse twenty-six goes to the root. Whoever trusts in his own mind is a fool, but he who walks in wisdom will be delivered. Matthew Poole says the fool is the man "who trusts to his own wit, neglecting or slighting the advice of others, and the counsel of God himself." Notice what is bundled together there. Trusting yourself and ignoring other people are the same act. That is why it connects to verse twenty-three. The person who cannot be rebuked has already decided his own mind is the final court.

Then verse twenty-seven turns from the mind to the hand. Whoever gives to the poor will not want, but he who hides his eyes will get many a curse. The first half is a promise, and Gill explains it modestly, saying the generous man "shall not be the poorer for what he gives." The second half is the surprising one. It does not say the one who refuses to give gets cursed for hating the poor. It says he hides his eyes. His sin is not seeing. And the chapter ends the way it began, with the wicked rising and people hiding, and when they perish the righteous increase.

Verse twenty-eight deserves a second look. When the wicked rise, people hide, but when they perish, the righteous increase. That is the same thing the chapter said in its first verse, bookending the whole section. Under bad rule, honest people go to ground, and the neighborhood gets quieter and poorer. Under a good one, they come out, and the place grows. Proverbs treats a community's life as something that depends on who is allowed to be seen. That is worth praying for in the people who govern us, not because politics is the center of life, but because the proverb says it shapes how safe it is to be good.

So here is the practical question. Is there an open hand and an open ear in your life this week? A closed hand and a closed ear are really the same condition, and both come from a mind that has decided it needs nothing and owes nothing. Give something away that costs you, and invite one honest correction. Then listen without defending.

Now to Luke twenty-four.

Luke has been telling us for chapters that the women were watching. Here is why. Verse one says on the first day of the week, at early dawn, they went to the tomb, taking the spices they had prepared. Early dawn is a vivid phrase in Greek, orthros batheos, something like the deep of the dawn. It is still very dark, and they are already on the road.

Luke has been careful about the timing. Back in chapter twenty-three he told us they rested on the Sabbath according to the commandment. They had spices ready and a grave they could find in the dark, and still they waited a full day, because God had said to rest. Their obedience cost them a day with a body they loved lying in a tomb. Nothing about that is dramatic. It is the quiet kind of faithfulness that keeps a commandment when it hurts, and it is exactly the kind Luke wants us to see standing at the front of the resurrection story.

Think about what that tells us. These women are not going to a tomb because they believe a promise. They are going because they love a dead man and they want to do the last thing for him that love can do. Matthew Henry puts it in a sentence worth keeping, "What is prepared for Christ, let it be used for him." There is a faithfulness that has no idea what is coming and keeps showing up anyway.

Verse two. They found the stone rolled away from the tomb. Verse three, they went in and did not find the body of the Lord Jesus. Luke says they were perplexed. Not joyful, not afraid yet. Perplexed. The Greek has the sense of being at a loss, with no road forward. They had a plan for a corpse, and the corpse was gone.

Then, suddenly, two men in dazzling clothes stood beside them. They bowed their faces to the ground, and the men said, why do you seek the living among the dead? He is not here, but has risen.

I want you to notice what the angels do next, because it is peculiar to Luke. They do not show the women anything. They do not say go look. They say remember. Remember how he told you, while he was still in Galilee, that the Son of Man must be delivered into the hands of sinful men and be crucified and on the third day rise. Ellicott observes that this direct appeal to the memory of the women is peculiar to Luke. Jesus had said it more than once, back in chapter nine and again on the road up to Jerusalem. The words had been there the whole time. They simply had not stuck. And verse eight says, and they remembered his words.

That is the hinge of the passage. The empty tomb alone could have meant a stolen body. It is the empty tomb together with his words that means resurrection. Faith here does not start with a feeling. It starts with remembering what was said.

Verse nine, they returned from the tomb and told all this to the eleven and to all the rest. And then Luke names them. Mary Magdalene, Joanna, and Mary the mother of James, and the other women with them. Joanna we met back in chapter eight, the wife of Chuza, who managed Herod's household. So there was a woman from inside Herod's own palace standing in the garden that morning. These are not anonymous. Luke wants their names on the record.

And Chrysostom, preaching on the same morning in Matthew's Gospel, makes a point that has not gone stale in sixteen hundred years. He says, "And these first see Jesus; and the sex that was most condemned, this first enjoys the sight of the blessings." The ones the world gave the least credit were the first to be told. And then he adds, "Let us men imitate the women; let us not forsake Jesus in temptations."

Now the hardest question in the passage, in verse eleven. These words seemed to them an idle tale, and they did not believe them. Why? The eleven had heard Jesus say this three times. Why would they dismiss it?

The word Luke chooses is leros. The Cambridge Bible says, "The strong word used leros implies mere nonsensical talk." Ellicott adds that it is a word used nowhere else in the New Testament, a word for the babble of someone who has lost their mind. And the Cambridge Bible notices the tense, which is the imperfect. It "shews persistent incredulity." They kept not believing. It was not one flinch. It was a settled refusal.

The Pulpit Commentary calls it remarkable, and it is. But think about the apostles' position. They had watched him die. They had watched the best man they ever knew executed as a criminal. Grief closes the ears as surely as pride does. They were not stupid. They were hurt. And hurt people protect themselves from hope, because hope is what hurt them last time. Gill says they took the women's report as "mere whims, and the fancies of their brains."

And then one man does something. Verse twelve. But Peter rose and ran to the tomb. The Cambridge Bible says it should be simply, but Peter arose, and that the little word but implies his readiness to believe. Everyone else stays where they are. Peter gets up. Matthew Henry says, "Peter now ran to the sepulchre, who but the other day ran from his Master." That is exactly the right thing to say. This is the man who denied him three times and fled into the night. He has no reason to expect a welcome. He runs anyway.

He stoops and looks in, and sees the linen cloths by themselves. Luke does not say Peter believed. He says he went home marveling at what had happened. Spurgeon preached on this morning in 1873, and he lingers on exactly what Peter saw, the grave clothes left behind. He says, "In the sweetest spiritual sense, our Lord Jesus has filled the grave with fragrance." The thing that terrified every human being about the grave is now a place with a rolled stone, folded linen, and no one in it.

So what do we do with this? The application is for the days when you have heard the promise, and still cannot make yourself believe it. Peter is the model there. He did not have faith yet. He had a willingness to go and look. If you are in a season where your prayers feel like an idle tale, you do not need to manufacture a feeling. You need to get up and go to the place where the evidence is. Open the book. Sit in the church. Do the thing, and let the marveling come.

Now Hebrews eleven.

We are at the end of the great chapter on faith, and the writer is running out of time. Verse thirty-two says, and what more shall I say? For time would fail me to tell of Gideon, Barak, Samson, Jephthah, of David and Samuel and the prophets. Six names, in a breath. These are not the polished heroes. Gideon was afraid, Barak would not go without Deborah, Samson was a wreck, and Jephthah made a rash vow. The writer picks the ones the book of Judges itself treats as flawed. Faith in this list is not a talent. It is a direction.

Then the verbs come fast. Through faith they conquered kingdoms, enforced justice, obtained promises, stopped the mouths of lions, quenched the power of fire, escaped the edge of the sword, were made strong out of weakness. That is the triumphant half. Daniel in the den, the three in the furnace. If the chapter ended here, it would be a victory parade.

But look at the middle of verse thirty-five. Women received back their dead by resurrection. That is the widow at Zarephath, and the Shunammite woman, whose sons Elijah and Elisha raised. The Cambridge Bible notes that these were resurrections to ordinary life, the child came back and would die again.

And then the sentence turns on one little word. Some were tortured, refusing to accept release, so that they might rise again to a better life. Notice that the first word of the second half is others, or in some translations, some. It is the same faith, and the outcome is the opposite. The ones in the first half were rescued. The ones in the second half could have been rescued, on terms, and refused. Release was offered, and the price was their faith. So they were beaten, the Greek is a word for being stretched on a drum or a wheel. They were mocked and flogged, chained and imprisoned, stoned, sawn in two, killed with the sword. They wandered in skins of sheep and goats, destitute, afflicted, mistreated, of whom the world was not worthy.

Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, hears that last phrase and cannot get past it. He quotes it and then reminds his congregation how little these people had. "They had not even raiment, he says, through the excess of affliction, no city, no house, no lodging-place." And yet, the verse says, the world was not worthy of them. The world thought it was passing a verdict on them. The writer says the verdict went the other way.

Do not hurry past what the writer has just done. He lists lions and furnaces next to sawn bodies and sheepskins, and he gives them the same grade. Nobody in the second half is told they lacked faith. Nobody in the first half is told they were softer. If you read this chapter hoping that enough faith will keep every bad thing from happening to you, verse thirty-five will not let you. Faith in Hebrews is not a method for getting rescued. It is a way of trusting God whether or not the rescue comes in this life.

Look at the phrase better resurrection. Better than what? Gill says, "The resurrection of the saints, which is unto everlasting life, is a better resurrection." The Cambridge Bible puts it as not a mere resurrection to earthly life, like the children of the women just mentioned. The boy from Zarephath got his life back. These martyrs refused to take a life that would end. They wanted the one that doesn't. That is what Hebrews means by better. It is the word this letter reaches for again and again, a better covenant, a better hope, a better country. Faith is not the refusal to want things. It is wanting the better thing so much that you can say no to the cheaper one.

Verses thirty-nine and forty are the sting, and the gift. All these, though commended through their faith, did not receive what was promised, since God had provided something better for us, that apart from us they should not be made perfect.

The great ones have not been paid yet. Chrysostom says, "They were before us as regards the conflicts, but are not before us as regards the crowns." And then, with a lovely tenderness, "For they also wait for the brethren." Abraham, Rahab, the nameless ones in the sheepskins, are waiting. Not impatiently. Waiting for you. The writer's point is that one family is being made complete, and nobody gets the final result before the rest. Gill says, "they and we make up but one church, and general assembly." Barnes says, "The fathers had one part in the promises, and we the other in the fulfullment."

Now remember why the letter says this. The readers are tired. They are being tempted to quit. And the writer says, look at the line of people who did not quit, and look at the fact that they are waiting on you. You are not running alone. Chapter twelve opens tomorrow with the word therefore, and you will see why.

So, the closing thoughts.

There is a real thread through all three readings, and it is about women and the dead. In Luke, the women come looking for a dead man and find that the dead are not where they expected. In Hebrews, women received back their dead by resurrection, and then the writer tells us that was only the smaller version of something coming. Luke twenty-four is the larger one. The grave that held every promise is open and empty, and the women who were trying to look after a corpse are the first to hear that he is not there.

And Proverbs is the quiet partner. The mind that trusts itself, the closed hand, the ear that cannot be corrected, those are all postures of someone who has no need of a rescue. The women at the tomb and the people in the sheepskins had a need, and they knew it.

So here is one question to carry through the day. Where have you stopped expecting anything, and what would it look like to get up and go and look, the way Peter did?

Tomorrow, on plan day nine twenty-two, we have Luke twenty-four, verses thirteen through twenty-seven, two travelers walking to Emmaus. Hebrews twelve, verses one through thirteen, where the great cloud of witnesses meets the race. And Proverbs twenty-nine, verses one through fourteen.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
