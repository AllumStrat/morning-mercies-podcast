Two people are walking away from Jerusalem on the afternoon of the third day. They are not running. They are not hiding. They are just walking, slowly, the way you walk when something you built your life around has fallen down. And a stranger falls into step beside them and asks what they are talking about. They stop in the road, and their faces are sad.

Good morning, Grace. This is Morning Mercies, and today is plan day nine twenty-two. We have three readings. Proverbs twenty-nine, verses one through fourteen. Luke twenty-four, verses thirteen through twenty-seven. And Hebrews twelve, verses one through thirteen.

Yesterday the angels at the empty tomb told the women to remember how Jesus had spoken to them, and the women remembered. Today we meet two people who did not. They had heard every word of it, and they are heading home with their hope in pieces. Hebrews, meanwhile, finishes its long roll call of faith and turns on a single word, therefore. And in Proverbs, a book that told us last time about the man who hardens his heart and the ear that refuses the law, we meet the neck that will not bend.

Let us start with Proverbs.

The twenty-ninth chapter is part of the collection that the men of Hezekiah copied out, and the opening verse is a hard one. He who is often reproved, yet stiffens his neck, will suddenly be broken beyond healing. Picture the image. John Gill says it plainly: the metaphor is taken from oxen, which kick and toss about. A farmer puts a yoke on the animal, and the animal throws its head and braces its neck against it. Notice the word often. This is not a man who has been corrected once and got angry. He has been reproved again and again. And notice the word suddenly. The refusal looks like strength for years. Then, in a moment, the neck that would not bend is broken, and the proverb says beyond healing. Not because God is stingy with mercy, but because the man has spent his whole life refusing the only medicine.

Hold that picture, because Hebrews will give us its opposite in about ten minutes.

Why does the chapter begin here? Because of where it sits. Chapters twenty-eight and twenty-nine are the last part of the collection that Hezekiah's men copied, and the editors have pressed the same themes together on purpose. Rulers and the poor. The righteous and the wicked. The one who listens and the one who will not. Yesterday we heard that whoever turns away his ear from hearing the law, even his prayer is an abomination. Today that same refusal gets a body, an ox with its neck set against the yoke.

Verses two through four are about what a ruler does to a whole people. When the righteous increase, the people rejoice, but when the wicked rule, the people groan. Verse three says a man who loves wisdom makes his father glad, but a companion of prostitutes squanders his wealth. Verse four says a king gives stability to the land by justice, but he who exacts gifts tears it down. Gifts there means bribes. A land does not stand on its army. It stands on whether a poor man can get a fair hearing.

That theme comes back at the end of our reading. Verse seven: a righteous man knows the rights of the poor, but a wicked man does not understand such knowledge. And verse thirteen is one of the most striking in the chapter. The poor man and the oppressor meet together; the Lord gives light to the eyes of both. Think about that. The oppressor and the oppressed stand in the same sunlight. God does not need to be flattered by the powerful or defended by the weak. He gives both of them their eyes, and he will ask both what they saw. Verse fourteen ends the thought. If a king faithfully judges the poor, his throne will be established forever.

Between those, there are three little warnings about the mouth.

Verse six adds a quiet contrast. An evil man is ensnared in his transgression, but a righteous man sings and rejoices. The wicked man is caught in a net of his own making, while the righteous man is free enough to sing. And verse ten says the bloodthirsty hate the blameless, and the upright seek his life. Goodness is not only ignored by wicked people. It is a standing rebuke to them, and that is why they hate it. Do not be surprised when a quiet, honest life draws more resentment than it seems to deserve. Verse five says a man who flatters his neighbor spreads a net for his feet. We usually think flattery is a gift, but the proverb says it is a trap, and the trap has two victims, the one flattered and the one flattering. Verse eight says scoffers set a city aflame, but the wise turn away wrath. Verse nine is the one I want you to remember. If a wise man has an argument with a fool, the fool only rages or laughs, and there is no quiet. Matthew Henry, who was never short of a vivid line, says it this way: he that fights with a dunghill, whether he be conqueror or conquered, is sure to be defiled. You can win that argument and still leave smelling of it.

Then verse eleven. A fool gives full vent to his spirit, but a wise man quietly holds it back. The Pulpit Commentary boils it down to a maxim: command your temper, lest it command you. Notice that the proverb does not say the wise man has no anger. He holds it back, which means he has something to hold. The difference between the wise man and the fool is not the feeling. It is the timing, and the door.

Put the chapter together and it is a single question. Who are you willing to be corrected by? The stiff neck in verse one, the flatterer in verse five, the fool who cannot hear in verse nine, and the ruler who listens to falsehood in verse twelve, all of them have arranged their lives so that no true word can reach them. So here is a practical thing for today. Think of one person who has told you something true that you did not enjoy hearing. Do not argue with it in your head again. Just ask yourself whether it was a yoke or a gift.

Now Luke.

We are on the road to Emmaus, a village about seven miles from Jerusalem. It is the same Sunday as the empty tomb. Luke tells us one of the two is named Cleopas, and the other is never named, which has led many readers to wonder whether it was his wife, or just someone Luke leaves for us to fill in. What matters is the direction. They are walking away from the city where the Messiah was supposed to be enthroned. Everything about the pace and the conversation says they are done.

Jesus himself draws near and walks with them, but their eyes were kept from recognizing him.

Think about the timing. By the afternoon of that Sunday, the news of the empty tomb has already been going around the disciples for hours. Peter has run to the grave. The women have spoken. And these two, hearing it all, do not wait to see what comes next. They leave. That tells us something honest about unbelief. It is rarely a lack of information. It is a decision about what to do with the information, and sometimes the decision is just to go home. Matthew Henry puts it simply: their eyes were held, that they should not know him. Luke does not say the disciples were dull, and he does not say Jesus was disguised. He says something held their eyes, and it was not by accident. Jesus, in the risen life, is going to teach before he is seen.

He asks, what are you discussing? And they stand still, looking sad. Cleopas is almost incredulous. Are you the only visitor to Jerusalem who does not know the things that happened there in these days? And Jesus says, what things? Hold on to that for a moment. The risen Lord asks to be told the story of his own death, by people who are walking away from it. He lets them say it out loud.

And they do. Jesus of Nazareth, a prophet mighty in deed and word, whom our chief priests and rulers delivered up to be condemned to death and crucified. Then comes the sentence at the center of the passage, verse twenty-one. But we had hoped that he was the one to redeem Israel. The Greek verb is elpizomen, we were hoping, in the tense of something that went on and on. We were hoping all the while. Notice what they have right. They know exactly who he was, and they know exactly what he did. They even know that some women have been to the tomb and found it empty, and that angels said he was alive.

Look at how they tell it. In verse twenty-four, some of those who were with us went to the tomb and found it just as the women had said, but him they did not see. That last clause is the whole trouble. They were willing to trust angels and women up to a point, but they needed to see him. Faith that waits for sight is the very thing Hebrews eleven spent a whole chapter taking apart, and you read it yesterday. They have every fact. What they do not have is the meaning. They have the news without the Bible.

So Jesus says, in verse twenty-five, O foolish ones, and slow of heart to believe all that the prophets have spoken! That sounds harsh. The Greek word is anoetoi, which means without understanding, not wicked, not mocking. Several of the old commentators have noticed that the sting is gentler than it sounds. Matthew Henry reads it as a plain charge: those are fools that act against their own interest. They were walking away from the one thing that could have mended them. Bengel, the eighteenth century German scholar, draws the lesson in one line: we ought to be quick in believing, where we have sufficient warrant of any word of God. The slowness is not in their heads. It is in their hearts. They believed some of what the prophets said, the glory and the kingdom, and they set aside the rest.

Then Jesus asks the question that turns the whole afternoon. Was it not necessary that the Christ should suffer these things and enter into his glory? The word is edei, it was necessary, it was binding. Not it was unfortunate. Not it was an accident that God made use of. The cross was in the script from the beginning, and glory was on the other side of it. These two walked away because they thought the suffering meant the story had failed. Jesus says it was the story.

And verse twenty-seven is what makes this passage so famous. Beginning with Moses and all the Prophets, he interpreted to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself. Matthew Henry notes the order: beginning at Moses, the first inspired writer of the Old Testament, he went in order through all the prophets. That is a whole afternoon, seven miles of walking, and the entire Old Testament as the lesson. We are not told which passages he used. Luke will not give us the notes. That is deliberate, I think, because he wants us to go and read the Old Testament and see for ourselves. The point is not a handful of proof texts. The point is that the whole shape of it, the promised seed, the sacrificed lamb, the suffering servant, the king who comes through death, was about him.

R.C. Sproul preached on this passage and began, this is one of the most glorious passages that we read anywhere in the New Testament. It is hard to disagree. And J.C. Ryle, who was a bishop and a patient pastor, makes an observation I find kind. There may be true grace hidden under much intellectual ignorance. Think about that. These two believed. They were Jesus' followers. And their understanding was in ruins. Ryle does not tell us to be careless about doctrine. He is a careful man, and he cares a great deal about getting it right. He tells us that confusion on a bad afternoon is not the same as unbelief, and that the Lord goes on walking with the confused.

A word on how Jesus teaches. He does not pull back the veil and say, it is I. He could have. Instead he opens the book. The first thing the risen Lord does for his discouraged friends is give them the Bible, rightly understood. Later in the chapter, when their eyes are opened, they say to one another, did not our hearts burn within us while he talked to us on the road? We will come to that tomorrow. For now, notice the order. Hearts first, then eyes. Do not wait to feel certain before you open the Scriptures with him.

So here is the application, and it comes straight out of the text. When Jesus asked what things, they told him. They did not tidy it. They did not say what a good disciple ought to feel. They said, we had hoped. If you are carrying a disappointment into today, a hope that did not happen, say it to him in those words. We had hoped. He will not be shocked. He is already walking beside you, and he has a way of answering a sad question with the Scriptures.

Now to Hebrews.

Chapter twelve begins with a therefore, and it is a big one. Chapter eleven gave us the roll call, Abel, Noah, Abraham, Moses, Rahab, and the unnamed people sawn in two. Now the author says, therefore, since we are surrounded by so great a cloud of witnesses, let us also lay aside every weight, and sin which clings so closely, and let us run with endurance the race that is set before us.

The word witnesses is martys. It does not mean spectators in the stands as much as it means people who have testified, whose lives said something. Several of the old commentators debate the picture. Some, like the nineteenth century scholar Meyer, say these are not onlookers at all. Others see a stadium. Either way, the point is encouragement. These people ran the same road, and they got home.

Then two things to throw off. A weight, and a sin. Notice they are different. A weight, as the commentators on Bible Hub point out, can be something that is perfectly allowable in itself, but that slows you. A runner does not run in a winter coat. And the sin that clings so closely, the Greek is a rare word that suggests something standing around you, easy to get at, always within reach. You know what yours is. Most of us do.

Verse two says the way to run is by looking to Jesus, the founder and perfecter of our faith. The word translated founder is archegos, a pioneer, one who goes first and opens the road. Perfecter is the one who brings it to the finish. Henry writes that he is not only the object, but the author. We are not asked to look at him as a trophy at the end of the track. He has already run the course ahead of us, who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross, despising the shame, and is seated at the right hand of the throne of God. Look at what he did with the shame. He despised it, he counted it small, because of the joy. That is how you are meant to run.

Verse three says to consider him who endured, so that you may not grow weary or fainthearted. Then the author does something pastoral. In your struggle against sin you have not yet resisted to the point of shedding your blood. These readers were suffering, but not yet to the grave. And he points them to a verse from Proverbs, which is a small delight for us today. Hebrews quotes the third chapter of Proverbs. My son, do not regard lightly the discipline of the Lord, nor be weary when reproved by him. For the Lord disciplines the one he loves, and chastises every son whom he receives.

Do you see the connection with our Proverbs reading? Proverbs twenty-nine opened with the man who is often reproved and stiffens his neck. Hebrews opens the other possibility. The same reproof, taken as a father's hand, is the sign that you are a son.

The Greek word is paideia, training, the whole upbringing of a child, not only the punishment. Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, reasons from the other direction. For if he scourges every son whom he receives, he who is not scourged, perhaps is not a son. That is a startling thing to hear. The absence of any correction would be the worrying sign. Matthew Henry agrees: the best of God's children need chastisement. And Spurgeon, preaching on this in 1855, said the rod has been baptized in deep affection before it is laid on the believer's back. Notice how all three are pointing the same way. Discipline is not God turning away from you. It is God staying.

Verses seven through ten push the comparison. Earthly fathers disciplined us for a short time as it seemed best to them, but God does it for our good, that we may share his holiness. Then verse eleven, the honest one. For the moment all discipline seems painful rather than pleasant, but later it yields the peaceful fruit of righteousness to those who have been trained by it. The author does not pretend it is pleasant. He says, it hurts, and there is a later. Henry writes that affliction produces peace, by producing more righteousness; for the fruit of righteousness is peace. And Chrysostom picks up the athlete image behind it. Chastisement is exercise, making the athlete strong, and invincible in combats, irresistible in wars. A man does not complain to his trainer that the training is hard. He understands that the hardness is the point.

The close, in verses twelve and thirteen, is practical. Lift your drooping hands and strengthen your weak knees, and make straight paths for your feet, so that what is lame may not be put out of joint but rather be healed. Someone in the group is limping. The straight path is for the weakest person, not the strongest. Whatever you do today, make the road a little smoother for someone who is limping.

One more thing about verse four. In your struggle against sin you have not yet resisted to the point of shedding your blood. It is easy to hear that as a rebuke, but it is also a quiet kindness. The writer is saying, your suffering is real, and it is also measurable. It has an edge. It will not go on without limit. A father who disciplines knows how much the child can bear.

Now let us bring the three together.

The two on the road to Emmaus and the readers of Hebrews have something in common. They are both in the middle of a hard stretch, and both are tempted to read it as proof that God has gone. The disciples thought the cross meant the Lord had lost. The Hebrews were tempted to think their suffering meant the Lord had left. And in both cases, the answer is that he is closer than they know. Jesus walks beside the two who cannot see him. The Father disciplines the child he will not let go. And Proverbs has been watching from the side with a question. When he comes alongside you, in a rebuke, in a stranger, in a hard word, will you bend your neck or stiffen it?

So here is the question to carry through the day. Where am I walking away from something, and who has God put beside me to turn me around?

Tomorrow we stay on the Emmaus road, as the two reach the village and the stranger seems to be going on, in Luke twenty-four, verses twenty-eight through thirty-five. We read Hebrews twelve, verses fourteen through twenty-nine, and Proverbs twenty-nine, verses fifteen through twenty-seven.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
