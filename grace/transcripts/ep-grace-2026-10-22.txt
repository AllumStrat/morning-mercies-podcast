Two soldiers are crouched in the dirt, and one of them is shaking a handful of something in his fist. They are gambling. Over their heads, nailed to a beam, hangs the man whose clothes they are fighting over. And in the middle of the noise, the dice, the jeering, the hammer still ringing in everyone's ears, that man opens his mouth. He does not curse them. He prays for them.

Here is what I want you to hold this morning. How can anyone ask for mercy for the very people who are doing this to him?

Welcome to Morning Mercies. We are at month nine, day seventeen of the Navigators plan, day two hundred and seventeen of three hundred, and today there are three readings. Proverbs twenty-six, verses seventeen through twenty-eight. Luke twenty-three, verses thirty-two through thirty-seven. And Hebrews ten, verses one through eighteen.

Yesterday we walked the road to the cross with Simon of Cyrene, and we listened to Jesus tell the weeping women of Jerusalem about the green wood and the dry. Today the road ends. They reach the place, and the nails go in. Yesterday Hebrews nine kept saying one little word, once. Christ offered himself once. Today Hebrews ten lets that word ring all the way to the end of the argument. So the Gospel is the heart of this episode again, but let's begin where we always do, in Proverbs.

Last time, Solomon's proverbs gave us the fool and the sluggard. These verses turn to something closer to home. Not the fool out there, but the quarrel, and the mouth that feeds it. Remember that these chapters were gathered by the scribes of King Hezekiah, so what we have is a carefully arranged collection, and you can see the arrangement here. Verses seventeen through twenty-two are about quarrels and the people who keep them burning. Verses twenty-three through twenty-six are about hatred that wears a pleasant face. And verses twenty-seven and twenty-eight close the chapter with a trap that snaps shut on the one who set it.

Verse seventeen: "Whoever meddles in a quarrel not his own is like one who takes a passing dog by the ears." Picture it. A stray dog is trotting past, minding its own business, and you grab it by the ears. You are the only one in the story who chose this. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, says the meddler "exposeth himself to great and needless hazards, as a man that causelessly provoketh a mastiff dog against himself." Needless is the key word. Matthew Henry says it more broadly: "To make ourselves busy in other men's matters, is to thrust ourselves into temptation." Notice, this is not a ban on peacemaking. Jesus blesses peacemakers. The proverb is about the person who has no business in the fight, and wades in anyway.

Then verses eighteen and nineteen take the most familiar excuse in the world and call it what it is. "Like a madman who throws firebrands, arrows, and death is the man who deceives his neighbor and says, I am only joking." Joking. You have heard it. You may have said it. The cruel comment, and then the laugh to take the edge off it. Solomon says that if the arrow lands, calling it a joke does not take it out of the wound.

Now the image in verses twenty through twenty-two, and it is a fire. Verse twenty: "For lack of wood the fire goes out, and where there is no whisperer, quarreling ceases." The Hebrew word for whisperer is nirgan, and it shows up only in Proverbs. It names the person who murmurs in corners, the one who passes along the thing that was told to him in confidence. Gill, the Baptist commentator, says that "Contention is like a fire, the flame of which is blown up by talebearers and whisperers." And Matthew Henry adds, "Contention heats the spirit, and puts families and societies into a flame." Verse twenty-one says a quarrelsome man is kindling for strife, and verse twenty-two explains why gossip spreads so well: "The words of a whisperer are like delicious morsels; they go down into the inner parts of the body." It tastes good. That is the trouble. Nobody passes on gossip because it is boring.

Here is the practical side of it, and it is wonderfully simple. A fire needs fuel. If there is no wood, it goes out. That means you and I are either wood or we are not. When someone starts telling you about the fight between two people you know, you do not have to argue with them or lecture them. You can simply not add wood. Do not repeat it. Do not text it onward. Ask whether you were part of the problem or part of the solution, and if neither, let it burn out.

Verses twenty-three through twenty-six turn from the whisperer to the flatterer. Verse twenty-three: "Like the glaze covering an earthen vessel are fervent lips with an evil heart." A cheap clay pot is dipped in a shiny glaze, and from across the room it looks like silver. Poole says of such a tongue and heart that they "are of no real worth, although sometimes they make a show of it, as dross doth of silver." Verses twenty-four and twenty-five warn you what to do when the speech is gracious, and the heart is not: do not believe him when he speaks graciously, because there are seven abominations in his heart. Smooth speech proves nothing. Verse twenty-six adds that even a hidden hatred, covered with deceit, will be exposed before the congregation. The disguise does not last forever. God sees through the glaze long before we do, and in the end everyone else does too. Watch what a person does when it costs him something.

And then the last two verses. Verse twenty-seven: "Whoever digs a pit will fall into it, and a stone will come back on him who starts it rolling." Henry looks at the schemer and marvels: "What pains men take to do mischief to others!" The sad joke is that all that labor is wasted. Verse twenty-eight says the lying tongue hates its victims, and the flattering mouth works ruin. Hold onto that picture of a man digging a pit and a stone rolling back downhill. It is going to meet us in a very surprising place.

Now to the Gospel, and I want us to slow down.

Luke twenty-three, verse thirty-two: "Two others, who were criminals, were led away to be put to death with him." Luke has already told us that on the night of the arrest, Jesus quoted Isaiah fifty-three: "And he was numbered with the transgressors." Here is that line in flesh and wood. Verse thirty-three: "And when they came to the place that is called The Skull, there they crucified him, and the criminals, one on his right and one on his left." The place is Golgotha in Aramaic, and Calvary in the Latin tradition, and both simply mean the skull. Notice how little Luke says about the crucifixion itself. No description of the nails, no details of the pain. Rome's readers knew that horror well enough. Luke is far more interested in what is said around the cross. And the first thing said is the most astonishing sentence in the chapter.

Verse thirty-four: "And Jesus said, Father, forgive them, for they know not what they do." A footnote in your Bible will tell you that a few early manuscripts leave out that sentence. The great majority include it, and it fits Luke's Gospel so completely, a Gospel of mercy to sinners, that the Church has treasured it from the beginning. Matthew Henry noticed the timing. "As soon as Christ was fastened to the cross, he prayed for those who crucified him." Not after the pain eased. Not at the end. The very moment the nails went in.

Look at who is praying. The Greek verb is in a form that suggests he kept saying it, over and over, while they worked. Father. Back in Luke eleven he taught his disciples to pray that way, and here he does it with a hammer in his ears. Now, the hardest question the verse raises. What does he mean, they know not what they do? Does ignorance excuse them? Are the soldiers off the hook because they were just following orders?

John Gill answers plainly: "not that their ignorance excused their sin." The prayer does not say, they are innocent. It says, forgive them. You only ask for forgiveness for people who need it. And Spurgeon, preaching on this very verse in October of 1890, pressed the point on his congregation. "The ignorance did not blot out the sin." Then he turned it on them, and on us: "But, dear friends, we might have known. If we did not know, it was because we would not know." The soldiers were not blind in the way a baby is blind. They were hardened. They had stopped asking who this was. That is how ignorance and guilt live in the same heart.

So the prayer is not a pardon handed out for free. It is an intercession, and intercessions get answered. Peter says to the crowd in Acts three, verse seventeen, "I know that you acted in ignorance, as did also your rulers." And Gill notes that about six weeks later, on the day of Pentecost, three thousand of the people in that city were pierced to the heart and baptized. Some of the very people who watched him die were forgiven because he asked.

Verse thirty-four goes on, and here is the moment from the cold open. "And they cast lots to divide his garments." David wrote that line a thousand years earlier in Psalm twenty-two, verse eighteen: "they divide my garments among them, and for my clothing they cast lots." Spurgeon, in that same sermon, noted the grim irony: "The gambling soldiers little dreamed that they were fulfilling the Scriptures while they were raffling for the raiment." Henry points out that the garment was worth so little that, divided up, it would come to next to nothing, and so they cast lots for it. A man's whole life is down to this, and the executioners are playing dice.

And now the mockery, which comes in three waves, and Luke wants you to count them. Verse thirty-five: "And the people stood by, watching, but the rulers scoffed at him, saying, He saved others; let him save himself, if he is the Christ of God, his Chosen One!" The Greek verb for scoffed is a vivid one. It means to turn up the nose, to sneer. The rulers, the men with the Scriptures in their hands, are the first. Matthew Henry caught the cruelty in it: "Thus was he upbraided for the good works he had done, as if it were indeed for these that they crucified him." They are using his mercy as the charge. And he truly did save others. That is the one thing they get right.

Verses thirty-six and thirty-seven: the soldiers come next, offering sour wine, which is cheap soldiers' drink and, if you know your Psalms, another echo. Psalm sixty-nine, verse twenty-one, says, "for my thirst they gave me sour wine to drink." They are offering it as a joke, and they are fulfilling Scripture again without knowing it. And they say, "If you are the King of the Jews, save yourself!" Tomorrow we will see a sign hung over his head, and one of the criminals join in. The same taunt three times. Save yourself. They could not have said anything truer, or more wrong. He could have saved himself. Remember what Matthew records him saying in Gethsemane, that he could ask his Father for more than twelve legions of angels. Remember too what he told the women only a few verses ago, that if they do this when the wood is green, what will happen when it is dry. The green wood is not struggling to get off the fire. He is lying down on it, for them. Henry has the answer: "They triumphed over him as if they had conquered him, whereas he was himself then more than a conqueror." The only way to save others was to refuse to save himself.

So where does the text leave you? With a prayer, and you can pray it today. Think of one person who has wounded you, someone who, if they stood in front of you and you asked, would say, I did not realize what I was doing. Now say the first half of the prayer for them, out loud if you can. Father, forgive them. You may not feel anything. He did not either, from what we can tell. He prayed it with the nails in.

Now to Hebrews ten. Hebrews chapters eight through ten, as we said yesterday, are the heart of the whole letter, and verses one through eighteen are where the argument lands. Everything the writer has said about the better priest, the better tent, and the better blood now comes to rest on one question. If the old sacrifices were from God, why did they never finish the job?

Verse one gives the answer in a picture. "For since the law has but a shadow of the good things to come instead of the true form of these realities, it can never, by the same sacrifices that are continually offered every year, make perfect those who draw near." A shadow tells you the shape of something, but you cannot lean on a shadow. John Chrysostom, preaching on this passage, saw the weakness in the repetition itself. He says, "For it is the excellence of a medicine to be applied once, and not often." If the medicine had worked, you would only need one dose. Instead, as verse three says, "in these sacrifices there is a reminder of sins every year." Every Day of Atonement told Israel, you are still guilty, come back next year. Chrysostom calls the whole system "an arraignment of weakness, not an exhibition of strength." John Piper says the same thing in his own words: "The prescribed repetition of sacrifices for sin in the Old Testament Law was a built-in testimony to their inadequacy."

Verse two makes the logic even sharper. If those sacrifices had really cleansed the worshipers, the writer says, would they not have stopped being offered? The people would no longer have had any consciousness of sins. Do you hear the echo of yesterday? Hebrews nine promised a blood that could purify the conscience. The conscience is the whole issue. A person can stand at an altar year after year and still walk home knowing the debt is unpaid.

Then verse four, flat and unsoftened: "For it is impossible for the blood of bulls and goats to take away sins." The animals were real, and the obedience was real, and God really commanded them. But a bull cannot pay for a person. The sacrifice that was needed had to be a person.

So in verses five through seven, the writer lets Christ speak, and he puts in his mouth Psalm forty. "Consequently, when Christ came into the world, he said, Sacrifices and offerings you have not desired, but a body have you prepared for me." That last phrase is worth a pause. The Hebrew of Psalm forty says something like, you have dug out ears for me, meaning, you have opened my ears to obey. The Greek translation that this writer is quoting reads, a body you have prepared. Either way the point is the same: God was never after the blood of animals for its own sake. He was after obedience, and only one man ever gave it completely. "I have come to do your will, O God."

And verse ten is the turn: "And by that will we have been sanctified through the offering of the body of Jesus Christ once for all." There it is again, the word from yesterday. Once for all. Sanctified here means set apart for God, and notice the tense. It is something that has been done to you, already, not something you are working toward.

Then the picture that makes the whole passage click. Verse eleven: "And every priest stands daily at his service, offering repeatedly the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins." Stands. There was no chair in the tabernacle. A priest's work was never done. "But when Christ had offered for all time a single sacrifice for sins, he sat down at the right hand of God." Matthew Poole says that Christ "ceased from sacrificing any more, and ascended up to heaven, and there he sat himself down." And Poole notes the contrast with the priests, who stood. Think what that means. The priest who stands has more to do. The priest who sits has finished.

Verse fourteen is the verse to memorize: "For by a single offering he has perfected for all time those who are being sanctified." The Greek word for perfected is teleioo, from telos, a goal or end. It means brought to the finish, made complete, fully qualified to stand in God's presence. John Piper explains that "Christ's people are perfected now in the sense that God puts away all our sin." That is what perfected means here. It does not mean you are sinless today. It means your standing is final. Notice the two tenses side by side, perfected for all time, and being sanctified. Your standing before God is finished, and your growth in holiness is still under way. Gill puts the first half firmly. Christ, he says, "has perfectly justified them from all things, and that for ever."

The writer then seals it with the new covenant promise from Jeremiah thirty-one, and quotes it a second time to be sure you heard it. Verse seventeen: "I will remember their sins and their lawless deeds no more." And verse eighteen draws the conclusion that you cannot argue with: "Where there is forgiveness of these, there is no longer any offering for sin." Think of how large that sentence is. Not less offering. No offering. The sacrifice has been made and received, and the priest has sat down.

Let me draw the threads together, because I think they are real this time.

Notice what the mockers said on the hill, three times over. Save yourself. They wanted him to come down and prove he was king. And what did Hebrews ten tell us? That he did not come down, that he stayed, and offered himself once, and sat down. If he had saved himself, the shadows would still be going. The bulls would still be bleeding. The reminder of sins would still come every year. His refusal to come down is the reason you can stop trying to earn your way back.

And there is a quieter connection with Proverbs. A man digs a pit, and a stone rolls back on the one who started it. The rulers dug their pit for Jesus. But by God's plan, the very cross they built became the place where their forgiveness was won. The proverb is true of all schemers, and it is true in the most astonishing way of this one.

So here is your question for the day. Hebrews says God remembers your sins no more. Luke shows Jesus asking God to forgive the people who nailed him up. Is there anyone you are still reminding of their sins, the way those sacrifices reminded Israel every year?

Tomorrow, Proverbs twenty-seven begins, with wisdom about tomorrow and flattery and friends. Luke takes us to the two criminals beside him and a plea to be remembered. And Hebrews ten moves from what Christ has done to what we are to do, with a call to draw near with confidence.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
