Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom. That is the whole prayer. Nine words in English. And it was prayed by a man with only a few hours of life left, nailed to a beam, with nothing to offer, no time to make anything right, and no one watching who thought it would matter. He did not ask to be taken down. He did not ask for a miracle. He asked to be remembered. And what he got back was one small word that changes everything. Today.

Good morning, Grace. This is Morning Mercies, and today is plan day nine eighteen. We have three readings. Proverbs twenty-seven, verses one through fourteen. Luke twenty-three, verses thirty-eight through forty-three. And Hebrews ten, verses nineteen through thirty-nine.

Yesterday we stood at the foot of the cross and listened to the taunt, save yourself. The rulers said it, the soldiers said it, and Luke tells us one of the criminals said it too. We ended by asking whether there is anyone you are still reminding of their sins. Today the scene does not move an inch, but the second criminal speaks, and he says something no one else on that hill will say. And Hebrews, which spent two chapters telling us what Christ has finished, finally turns and says, therefore, let us. So we begin in Proverbs, where Solomon has a few things to say about tomorrow, and about the kind of friend who tells you the truth.

Proverbs twenty-seven sits in the long section that begins at chapter twenty-five, verse one, which says these are more proverbs of Solomon that the men of Hezekiah king of Judah copied. So about two hundred and fifty years after Solomon, a team of scribes gathered up his sayings and set them in order. That is why the chapters read like a drawer of tools more than a speech. Verse by verse, you are handed one clear thing at a time, and the skill is in noticing how they sit beside each other.

The chapter opens with a proverb about time. Verse one. Do not boast about tomorrow, for you do not know what a day may bring. Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, makes a careful point. He says, "This does not forbid preparing for to-morrow, but presuming upon to-morrow." The wise farmer still plants. What the proverb forbids is the confident sentence that begins, next year I will. James picks this up in the New Testament, and Jesus tells of a rich man whose barns were full and whose soul was demanded that night. John Gill, with his usual thoroughness, piles up the evidence, and his sentence is the plainest of all. No man, he says, "can assure himself of more than the present time." Hold onto that. We will hear it again in Luke.

Verse two is the same lesson from another side. Let another praise you, and not your own mouth. If tomorrow is not yours, neither is your reputation. Leave it with others.

Then verses three and four turn to weight and heat. A stone is heavy and sand is weighty, but a fool's provocation is heavier than both. And then comes a surprise. Wrath is cruel, anger is overwhelming, but who can stand before jealousy? Anger burns and passes. Jealousy keeps its grudge. Notice that the proverb ranks them. It is warning you about the quiet resentment more than the loud temper. A man who shouts is easy to see coming. A man who smiles at you and keeps a ledger is not.

The middle of the chapter is about friendship, and it gets sharper. Verse five. Better is open rebuke than hidden love. Verse six. Faithful are the wounds of a friend, profuse are the kisses of an enemy. Henry says that "Plain and faithful rebukes are better, not only than secret hatred, but than love which compliments in sin, to the hurt of the soul." Read that slowly. He is saying that flattery can be a form of hatred wearing love's clothes. A friend who sees you walking toward a cliff and says nothing has not loved you. The wound is faithful because it is meant to heal.

Verse seven is a little picture. One who is full loathes honey, but to one who is hungry everything bitter is sweet. When you are satisfied with yourself, even sweet counsel tastes thin. When you are hungry, hard words go down as food. Verse nine says oil and perfume make the heart glad, and the sweetness of a friend comes from his earnest counsel. Notice that Solomon has not said the friend is the one who agrees with you. He has said the opposite. So the chapter has already told you what a friend is. A friend is the one whose advice is sweet even when it stings.

Verse ten says do not forsake your friend and your father's friend, and do not go to your brother's house in the day of your calamity. Better is a neighbor who is near than a brother who is far away. Matthew Poole reads nearness as a matter of the heart, a neighbor near "in affection, in which respect God is oft said to be near to the righteous, and far from the wicked." And Henry, in his short note, cannot resist the thought that follows. "But there is a Friend that sticketh closer than a brother, and let us place entire confidence in him."

The last verses are quick strokes. Verse twelve, the prudent sees danger and hides himself, but the simple go on and suffer for it. Verse fourteen is almost funny. Whoever blesses his neighbor with a loud voice, rising early in the morning, will be counted as cursing. Flattery that is too loud, too early and too eager sounds like a hook. So here is something to carry out of Proverbs. Ask yourself who in your life is allowed to wound you faithfully, and whether you would let them.

Now to Luke. We are in the middle of the crucifixion, and Luke has been painting it in careful strokes. Verse thirty-eight says there was also an inscription over him, this is the King of the Jews. The Romans meant it as mockery, a sign nailed up to tell passersby what happens to men who claim a throne. Luke wants you to notice that they have written the truth without knowing it, and that the whole scene which follows is a conversation about that very sign. Is he a king, and what does his kingdom look like? Then come the taunts, and verse thirty-nine says one of the criminals who were hanged railed at him, saying, are you not the Christ? Save yourself and us. Matthew and Mark say both men joined the mockery. Luke, who alone gives us the rest of the scene, lets us see that one of them stopped.

Verse forty. The other rebuked him, saying, do you not fear God, since you are under the same sentence of condemnation? Hear what he does first. He does not speak to Jesus. He speaks to his fellow sufferer, and he speaks about God. John Calvin takes the question to mean that even in the very jaws of death this man will not fear God. Calvin sees three proofs of repentance in the man, and they come in order. He rebukes his companion. He admits his own guilt. And he entrusts himself to Christ.

Verse forty-one is the confession. And we indeed justly, for we are receiving the due reward of our deeds, but this man has done nothing wrong. Notice there is no excuse in that sentence. No one else was to blame, and no mitigating circumstance. And it contains a witness to Jesus that Pilate, Herod and the rulers never gave. This man has done nothing wrong.

Then verse forty-two. Jesus, remember me when you come into your kingdom. The best manuscripts have just the name, Jesus, a man speaking plainly to a man he trusts. Augustine, as the Cambridge Bible notes, put it neatly. Mercy prayed, so that misery might pray. In other words, the thief had just heard Jesus ask the Father to forgive the men who were killing him, and a prayer like that makes it possible for a lost man to pray.

Think about what the thief believed. He is looking at a king whose throne is a cross, whose crown is thorns, whose subjects are mocking him, and he says, when you come into your kingdom. John Gill says, "This was great faith indeed to be exercised on Christ at such a time as this, when he was under the greatest reproach and ignominy." Calvin goes further than anyone. "I know not that, since the creation of the world, there ever was a more remarkable and striking example of faith." And he explains why. The disciples who had walked with Jesus for three years had scattered. This man, with no teaching, no miracles to lean on and a body already half destroyed, "beheld life in death, exaltation in ruin, glory in shame, victory in destruction, a kingdom in bondage."

Now verse forty-three. Truly, I say to you, today you will be with me in paradise. Three small words are worth a pause. First, truly. This is the Greek amen, the word Jesus uses when he is about to say something he will stake his name on. Second, today. The Greek is semeron. The man asked for a remembrance someday, in the kingdom, and Jesus answers with this very day. There is a scholarly question about whether to put today with the verb say or with the verb will be. But Jesus does not say, I say to you today. The promise is that today you will be. John Gill notices that Jesus promised him more than he asked for, and sooner than he expected. He asked to be remembered, and he was given company.

The third word is paradise. It is a Persian word. It meant an enclosed park or garden for a king, and the Greek translators of the Old Testament used it for the garden of Eden. So Jesus is saying that the king is taking a thief back into the garden. A Ligonier article on this verse makes the point that matters most for the dying. It says, "Our eternal salvation is immediate upon death because Jesus’ work on the cross is sufficient." The thief did nothing. He could do nothing. He was nailed hand and foot.

Spurgeon preached on this text in April of 1889, in a sermon called The Believing Thief. And he says what everyone should say about faith like this. "Trust is the saving act." The man did not climb a ladder of good works. He trusted. And Spurgeon loves the speed of it. "The stress of the text lies in the speediness of all this." In a few hours, he says, "the beggar was lifted from the dunghill and set among princes."

Now here is the hard question, and I would rather you hear it from me than wonder about it quietly. Does this make repentance a thing you can put off? If a man can be saved at the very end, why not wait? Matthew Henry answers it with a sentence that has been repeated for three hundred years. "It is certain that true repentance is never too late; but it is as certain that late repentance is seldom true." The thief is not a loophole. He is a rescue. Henry calls it a single instance in Scripture, one that should teach us to despair of none, and that none should despair of themselves. But lest it be abused, he says, Luke sets beside it the other thief, who died hardened with a Savior so near. And remember Proverbs, which we have just read. Do not boast about tomorrow. The thief had no tomorrow, and neither do you have one you can count on.

There is also a quiet comfort in Calvin's last note on the passage. The promise did not take away the man's pain. His cross was still his cross that afternoon. Calvin says that "This reminds us that we ought not to judge of the grace of God by the perception of the flesh." And on the other side he says, "it is impossible that he shall forget those who commit to him their salvation."

So the application is small and it is yours. Take the thief's prayer as your own, today, and not as a last resort. It has nothing about your record in it. It has Jesus, it has remember, and it has a kingdom. If you have prayed it, then listen to the answer. Today. Not someday when you are better.

Now the letter to the Hebrews. We have spent three days in the center of this book's argument, from chapter eight through ten, verse eighteen. Christ is the better priest with the better sacrifice in the better tent, and he has sat down because the work is finished. Chapter ten, verse nineteen, begins with the word therefore. Everything the writer has said now turns into something to do. And the verbs are in a group of three, all beginning with let us. Let us draw near, verse twenty-two. Let us hold fast, verse twenty-three. Let us consider how to stir up one another, verse twenty-four.

Look at what the first one rests on. Since we have confidence to enter the holy places by the blood of Jesus, by the new and living way that he opened for us through the curtain, that is, through his flesh. Any Jewish reader would have felt that sentence in the stomach. Only the high priest went behind the curtain, once a year. Now the writer says you have confidence to go in. John Chrysostom, preaching on this verse, notes that the curtain itself becomes a picture. "For when it was lifted up on high, then the things in heaven appeared." The flesh of Christ was torn open, and through it we see where we were going all along. And, by the way, tomorrow in Luke the curtain of the temple itself tears from top to bottom. Today, in Luke, a thief walks through it ahead of time.

Verse twenty-two says draw near with a true heart in full assurance of faith, with our hearts sprinkled clean from an evil conscience and our bodies washed with pure water. Chrysostom reads the sprinkling and the washing as two halves. God cleanses the conscience. We come honestly and in faith. The Reformed church has long heard in this the Lord's own work of cleansing, with baptism as the sign, and Baptist friends hear the same cleansing in the faith of the one who comes.

Verse twenty-three says hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering, for he who promised is faithful. Your hold is only as good as the one who holds you, and the reason is in the second half of the verse. He is faithful. The writer does not tell them to grip harder because they are strong. He tells them to grip because the one who made the promise has never once let go of his end of it.

Then verse twenty-four and twenty-five are about church. Let us consider how to stir up one another to love and good works, not neglecting to meet together, as is the habit of some, but encouraging one another, and all the more as you see the Day drawing near. The Greek for stir up is a strong word, the same root as the English paroxysm. It is a word of provocation, but here the provocation is to love. Chrysostom says, "He knew that much strength arises from being together and assembling together." And he has an image I like very much. "For if a stone rubbed against a stone sends forth fire, how much more soul mingled with soul!" Notice that this is why the writer cares about meeting together. Not because it is a rule, but because Christians who are alone grow cold.

Then the temperature of the letter drops. Verse twenty-six. For if we go on sinning deliberately after receiving the knowledge of the truth, there no longer remains a sacrifice for sins, but a fearful expectation of judgment. Verse twenty-nine asks who has trampled underfoot the Son of God and profaned the blood of the covenant. And verse thirty-one says it is a fearful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. This passage has frightened sincere Christians for centuries, so let me say what the tradition says. Calvin notes that the early Novatians used it to say that anyone who sinned after baptism could never be forgiven, and he says they were wrong. Calvin writes, "Those who sin, mentioned by the Apostle, are not such as offend in any way, but such as forsake the Church, and wholly alienate themselves from Christ." The sin is not stumbling. It is walking away from the one sacrifice and treating its blood as common. Notice the grammar too. It says go on sinning, a settled and deliberate pattern of rejection. If you are troubled by your sin and want to be rid of it, you are not the person described. You are the one the first half of the chapter was written for.

Verses thirty-two through thirty-four are the writer's way of coaxing them back. Recall the former days. These Christians had been publicly insulted, jailed, and robbed, and they had accepted the plundering of their property with joy, since they knew they had a better possession and an abiding one. That is what faith looks like when it is working. So verse thirty-five says do not throw away your confidence, which has a great reward. Verse thirty-six says you have need of endurance. Calvin puts it frankly. "Christians will never advance two paces without fainting, except they are sustained by patience."

And then in verses thirty-seven and thirty-eight he quotes Habakkuk. Yet a little while, and the coming one will come and will not delay, but my righteous one shall live by faith, and if he shrinks back, my soul has no pleasure in him. Habakkuk had been told Babylon was coming against his own people, and he was told to wait for a vision that seemed slow. Hebrews takes that waiting and gives it a name. The coming one is Christ, and the delay is shorter than it feels.

Verse thirty-nine is where the writer lands. But we are not of those who shrink back and are destroyed, but of those who have faith and preserve their souls. He includes himself. We. Not you must, but we are.

Let me draw a few threads together, because I think they are real. The thief prayed, remember me. And yesterday Hebrews quoted the promise, I will remember their sins no more. Those two belong together. He asked to be remembered, and God remembers him by forgetting everything against him. Second, Proverbs said do not boast about tomorrow, and Jesus said to the thief, today. Third, notice that the thief did what Proverbs calls faithful. He wounded his fellow criminal with the truth, and Hebrews asks the same of us. Stir one another up. And last, Hebrews says we have confidence to enter through the curtain. The thief went in before the curtain tore, and we go in after.

So here is your question for the day. If you were given only today, what would you ask the Lord to remember you for? Not your record, but the prayer. And is there someone you could faithfully wound with the truth, in love?

Tomorrow we reach the moment of the cross itself. Luke twenty-three, verses forty-four through forty-nine, with the darkness and the tearing of the curtain. Hebrews eleven begins its great chapter on faith. And Proverbs twenty-seven finishes with a quarrelsome wife, iron sharpening iron, and the care of the flock.

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
