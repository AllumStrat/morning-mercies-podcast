Came to the Light, and came in the darkness.

That is how Augustine described a man named Nicodemus, sixteen hundred years ago, and I cannot get the line out of my head. A respected teacher, a man with a seat on the council, slips through the streets of Jerusalem after the lamps are lit, because he has a question he does not want anyone to hear him ask. And the answer he gets is not what he came for. It is stranger, and it is better.

Good morning, Grace, and welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day eight of month ten, and today we have three readings. Ecclesiastes, chapter six. John, chapter three, verses one through fifteen. And James, chapter four, verses one through ten.

Yesterday we watched Jesus clear the temple, and then we read a sentence that was easy to miss. Many believed in his name when they saw the signs, but Jesus did not entrust himself to them, because he knew what was in man. John does not leave that sentence hanging for long. The very next verse introduces a man, a Pharisee, a ruler of the Jews, and the Greek even repeats the word for man. So here is what is in man, standing in front of Jesus in the dark. And James, yesterday, talked about the bitter envy and selfish ambition that sit under our displays. Today he asks where all our fighting comes from. Let us begin with the Preacher.

Ecclesiastes, chapter six.

A word about where we are in the book. Chapters one through six form the first half of Ecclesiastes, and chapter six is its closing note. The Preacher has tested pleasure, work, wisdom, and wealth, and in each one found the same thing, that nothing under the sun can be held. The Hebrew word he keeps using is hebel, which means breath, or vapor. It is the steam from a kettle, something you can see and cannot grasp. Chapter six is the last long look at that vapor before the book turns, in chapter seven, to what a wise person does with it.

Last time the Preacher closed chapter five with good news. When God gives a person wealth and possessions and the power to enjoy them, that is a gift. Chapter six opens with the dark twin of that verse. There is an evil that I have seen under the sun, and it lies heavy on mankind. The key phrase is in verse two. God gives a man wealth, possessions, and honor, so that he lacks nothing of all that he desires, yet God does not give him the power to enjoy them.

Read that slowly. Nothing is missing. Every wish is met. And the man still cannot enjoy a bite of it. A stranger enjoys it instead. The Preacher calls this vanity, and he calls it a grievous evil.

Notice who is doing the giving and the withholding. The power to enjoy is not something you can buy with the wealth itself. It is its own gift, held in another hand. Matthew Poole, the Puritan, says that the man is left to himself, and puts it sharply. God gives him up to a base and covetous mind. In other words, the problem is not the bank account. It is the heart that holds it, a heart that cannot say enough.

Then the Preacher makes his famous comparison, and it is a hard one. Verses three through six. A man might father a hundred children and live many years, and yet if his soul is not satisfied with life's good things, and he has no burial, then a stillborn child is better off than he. The child comes in vanity and goes in darkness, and has never seen the sun, yet has more rest than that man. Even if the man lived two thousand years, but did not enjoy anything good, do not all go to the one place?

We should be honest. This is shocking, and the Preacher means it to be. He is not saying that a stillborn child has a good life, or that grieving parents should feel anything but sorrow. He is using the most extreme comparison he can find to say one thing. A long life with a hundred children and a full barn, but without any satisfaction, is not a blessing. Length and quantity are not what make a life good. The question is whether the person can receive what is given with a thankful heart.

Verse seven is one of the most quotable lines in the book. All the toil of man is for his mouth, and yet his appetite is not satisfied. People work to eat, and then they are hungry again. The Preacher is not laughing at eating. He is pointing out that appetite is a hole that never stays full, and that a life spent only feeding it has no end point. Then verse eight asks what advantage the wise man has over the fool, and what advantage the poor man has in knowing how to conduct himself before the living. The answer is hidden in the question. Neither one escapes the end.

The chapter ends with two verses that I think are the practical heart of it. Verse nine says, better is the sight of the eyes than the wandering of desire. That word, wandering, is the picture of a man whose appetite roams far from home. John Gill reads the verse this way. It is better to enjoy contentedly things in sight and in possession. The thing in front of you, your table, your work, your people, is real. The thing you are chasing in your head is not. And the last verses remind us that whatever has come to be has already been named, and that no one can dispute with someone stronger than he. Who knows what is good for a person in life, the few days of his vain life, which he passes like a shadow? Who can tell him what will be after him under the sun?

So here is the question the Preacher leaves with us. Not, do you have enough? But, can you receive what you have? That is a different problem, and money cannot solve it. Only God can, and he does it by giving contentment, which is a gift as much as the food on the plate. If you notice that you are always reaching past today for the next thing, ask him for that. Ask him for the power to enjoy what he has already put in your hands.

John, chapter three, verses one through fifteen.

This is one of the best known conversations in the Bible, so let us slow down and see how John sets it up. Chapter two ended with Jesus not trusting himself to a crowd that liked his signs. Chapter three begins, now there was a man of the Pharisees named Nicodemus, a ruler of the Jews. He comes to Jesus by night. He says, Rabbi, we know that you are a teacher come from God, for no one can do these signs that you do unless God is with him.

Look at his language. We know. He speaks for a group, probably other members of the council who are impressed and nervous. He is polite. He is even generous. He gives Jesus the highest title his world allowed a teacher. And he says it at night.

A word on who Nicodemus was. The council, called the Sanhedrin, had seventy members and the high priest, and it held the highest authority the Jews had under Rome. A Pharisee on that council had spent decades learning the law line by line. This was no seeker wandering in off the street. This was an expert coming to a carpenter's son from Galilee, which tells you how much the signs at Passover had unsettled Jerusalem.

Why night? Some say it was the usual time for serious study, since rabbis taught late. Some say he was afraid of being seen. Augustine, in his tractate on this passage, will not soften it. He says that Nicodemus came to the Lord, and came by night. Came to the Light, and came in the darkness. And in John's Gospel, night is never only a time on the clock. Remember what the Gospel said at the beginning, that the light shines in the darkness. Nicodemus is a man standing in that darkness, near the Light, but not yet in it.

And now notice what Jesus does. Nicodemus has asked no question at all. He has only made a compliment. And Jesus answers as though he had heard what lay under it. Truly, truly, I say to you, unless one is born again he cannot see the kingdom of God.

Here is the key word of the chapter. Born again. The Greek word is anothen, and it has two meanings. It can mean again, a second time. And it can mean from above. John loves a word that carries two meanings at once, and here both are true. A birth from above is a second birth. Nicodemus hears the first meaning only, and he says, how can a man be born when he is old? Can he enter a second time into his mother's womb? He is not stupid. He is a teacher who is hearing something that has no category in his training.

John Calvin sees what Jesus is claiming. He says that by the phrase born again is expressed not the correction of one part, but the renovation of the whole nature. Do you hear what that rules out? Nicodemus did not need a tune up. He was a moral man by any measure, a student of the law, a leader of his people. Jesus does not say, improve your life. He says, you must be born. You cannot make yourself. Nobody gave themselves their first birth, and nobody gives themselves the second.

Then comes verse five, which people have argued about for centuries. Unless one is born of water and the Spirit, he cannot enter the kingdom of God. Augustine took the water to mean baptism, as did much of the early church. Many in the Reformed tradition, including Calvin, take water and Spirit together as one picture of cleansing, drawn from Ezekiel thirty-six, where God promises to sprinkle clean water on his people and put a new spirit in them. Jesus has just called Nicodemus the teacher of Israel, and in verse ten he is shocked that Nicodemus does not know these things. That tells you the background is the Old Testament promise, not a new ceremony. Whichever way you take the water, the weight of the sentence falls on the Spirit. Flesh gives birth to flesh. Spirit gives birth to spirit. Verse six.

And then the line that most of us have heard in a hundred sermons. The wind blows where it wishes, and you hear its sound, but you do not know where it comes from or where it goes. So it is with everyone who is born of the Spirit.

There is a wordplay here that English hides. The Greek word pneuma means wind, and it also means spirit, the same word for both. So Jesus can say, the pneuma blows where it wills, and every hearer catches both meanings. You cannot see the wind. You can see the leaves move. You cannot see regeneration, but you can see a life turn.

Spurgeon preached a whole sermon on this verse in 1877, and he put the matter in a sentence. We cannot control nor command the Spirit of the living God, he is in the highest sense a free agent. Think what that does to Nicodemus. He came with respectful words and good credentials, as a man who has something to offer. Jesus tells him that the thing he needs is not on his shelf and not in his power. It comes as the Spirit wills. A Ligonier devotional on this passage says it honestly. We know that he works, but we cannot always explain how he works. And that is right. Jesus himself says in verse eight that you cannot map the wind. What you can do is stop standing in the way, and ask.

Nicodemus says, how can these things be? And Jesus answers with a gentle jab. Are you the teacher of Israel and yet you do not understand these things? Then he shifts from the wind to a higher register. We speak of what we know. If I have told you earthly things and you do not believe, how will you believe if I tell you heavenly things? He has just talked about birth and wind, things on earth that we can see. If those are hard, what about what is in heaven? And the only one who could tell you is the one who came down.

Then the last two verses, and they are the hinge of the whole chapter. As Moses lifted up the serpent in the wilderness, so must the Son of Man be lifted up, that whoever believes in him may have eternal life.

Back in the book of Numbers, the people grumbled, and poisonous snakes came into the camp. God told Moses to make a bronze serpent and put it on a pole, and anyone who looked at it lived. Not one of them was told to explain it. They just had to look. Augustine puts the paradox in a single line. The serpent's bite was deadly, the Lord's death is life-giving. The very thing that looked like a curse became the cure. And notice the word must. So must the Son of Man be lifted up. It is not an accident that the cross happens. It is the plan.

Here is the one application I want to leave you with. Grace, if you are tired of trying to make yourself into a better person, listen to what Jesus tells the best man in Jerusalem. You cannot. You must be born, and then you must look. Nicodemus, as we will see later in this Gospel, turns up again at the cross, bringing spices for a burial. The man who came by night ends up in the afternoon sun at the foot of a lifted up Savior. That is how the wind blows.

James, chapter four, verses one through ten.

James is a book of short, sharp strokes, and chapter four is one of the sharpest. Yesterday we heard about two wisdoms, one from below, bitter and selfish, and one from above, peaceable and gentle. Now James names what the first wisdom does in real communities. What causes quarrels and what causes fights among you? Is it not this, that your passions are at war within you?

The Greek here uses the word for pleasures, hedonai, which is where we get hedonism. The picture is a battlefield inside every one of us, and the shooting spills out into the church, the family, the workplace. James does not say that the problem is out there, in difficult people. He says it is in here. You desire and do not have, so you murder. You covet and cannot obtain, so you fight and quarrel. Then he gives two stunning reasons why prayer does not work for such people. You do not have, because you do not ask. You ask and do not receive, because you ask wrongly, to spend it on your passions.

Remember where we were in Ecclesiastes this morning. Wandering desire. A man who has everything and cannot enjoy it. James is describing the same disease from inside. Desire that cannot be satisfied turns into a fight.

To hear James properly, remember who he is. He is the brother of Jesus, the leader of the church in Jerusalem, and his letter is written to Jewish Christians scattered abroad, people who knew trouble. James does not lecture outsiders. He speaks to believers about how the old wisdom from below still pulls at them. Which is why his tone is so direct.

Then verse four comes like a slap. You adulterous people. Friendship with the world is enmity with God. Why adulterous? Because in the Old Testament, God's people are his bride, and idolatry is infidelity. Matthew Henry says it flatly. Worldly-mindedness is enmity to God. And he adds a line that I find chilling. An enemy may be reconciled, but enmity never can be reconciled. John Gill puts the same thought in plain terms, that God and mammon cannot be loved and served by the same persons, at the same time. That is not James adding a new rule. It is Jesus in the Sermon on the Mount, where he said you cannot serve God and money.

Verse six is the turn, and it is the best news in the passage. But he gives more grace. Therefore it says, God opposes the proud, but gives grace to the humble. James is quoting Proverbs three, verse thirty-four. And the old German commentator Bengel puts it in a sentence you could memorize. He resists the proud, but he gives grace to the lowly.

The word for oppose is a military one. God lines up against the proud like an army. But the very same verse says that he gives more grace. Look at the logic. The war inside us is real. The pull of the world is real. But God's supply is larger than all of it. It is more grace, not just enough grace. And it flows to the one who admits he is empty.

Then follows a run of ten commands, almost like a drumbeat. Submit yourselves therefore to God. Resist the devil, and he will flee from you. Draw near to God, and he will draw near to you. Cleanse your hands, you sinners, and purify your hearts, you double-minded. Be wretched and mourn and weep. Let your laughter be turned to mourning and your joy to gloom. Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you.

Notice the order. You submit first, and then you resist. A lot of us try to resist the devil in our own name, and we lose. James says submit to God, and the devil has nothing to push against. Notice also how God moves. You draw near, and he draws near. That is not a bargain. It is how a father responds to a child who has come back to the table. And the language of mourning is not gloom for its own sake. James is calling for honest grief over sin, the kind that Nicodemus never had to feel until he heard that he needed a new birth.

And the last line is a promise. Humble yourselves before the Lord, and he will exalt you. Not you exalting yourself, and not the world exalting you. He will. Grace, that is the same direction as the wind. You cannot lift yourself. You can only come low, and be lifted.

Let me close by tying these three together, because I think the thread is real.

The man in Ecclesiastes has everything he reaches for and enjoys none of it. Nicodemus has every credential he could want and cannot see the kingdom. The people in James have every desire and fight with one another. In each case, the trouble is not that they lack something to grasp. It is that they are trying to take what can only be given. Joy is given. New birth is given. Grace is given, and more of it, to the humble. The wind blows where it wishes, and you can only open the window.

So here is the question to carry through the day. What am I reaching for today that God would rather give me? Take it to him once in the morning, and once more in the afternoon, and see whether your hands loosen.
