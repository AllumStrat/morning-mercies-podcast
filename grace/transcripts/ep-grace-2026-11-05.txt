Can anything good come out of Nazareth?

It is a sneer, and it is thrown out in the first chapter of John by a man who is about to become one of the most honest believers in the whole Gospel. And the answer he gets is three words long. Come and see. Hold on to that, because today everything turns on whether we can come, and see, and then live what we have seen.

Good morning, Grace, and welcome to Morning Mercies. This is plan day five of month ten, and today we have three readings. In Ecclesiastes, chapter three, verses sixteen through twenty-two. In John, chapter one, verses forty-three through fifty-one. And in James, chapter two, verses fourteen through twenty-six.

Yesterday Jesus turned around and asked two followers what they were looking for, and then he said, come and you will see. Today Philip takes that same invitation and hands it to a friend. And in James, we left off with mercy triumphing over judgment, and today James asks what kind of faith produces mercy at all. But first, the Preacher, who yesterday told us that God makes everything beautiful in its time. Today he looks at the places where nothing looks beautiful at all.

Ecclesiastes three, verses sixteen through twenty-two.

The Preacher has just given us the poem of the times, and now he turns his head and looks at the courthouse. Verse sixteen says, moreover, I saw under the sun that in the place of justice, even there was wickedness, and in the place of righteousness, even there was wickedness. Notice the repetition. Not that wickedness exists in the world, which nobody doubts. It is that wickedness has taken the very seat where justice is supposed to sit. The judge is crooked. The one place you counted on is the place that fails.

This is a wise man, an honest one, and he does not look away. Many of us have stood in a smaller version of that moment. A person who should have protected you did not. An institution that promised fairness handed you a form letter. The Preacher does not tell you that this is secretly fine. He says he saw it.

Then verse seventeen gives the first answer. I said in my heart, God will judge the righteous and the wicked, for there is a time for every matter and for every work. Here is where the poem of the times pays off. The same God who has a time to plant and a time to pluck up has a time for judgment. Matthew Henry, in his concise commentary, puts it this way. Quote. With God there is a time for the redressing of grievances, though as yet we see it not. End quote. That last clause is the honest part. Though as yet we see it not. The Preacher is not claiming that the books are balanced today. He is claiming that there is a Judge, and that the date is set.

But the Preacher does not stop with comfort, because he is too honest. Verse eighteen says that God is testing the children of man to show them that they are but beasts. That is a startling sentence. And the next verses explain it. For what happens to the children of man and what happens to the beasts is the same. As one dies, so dies the other. They all have the same breath, and man has no advantage over the beasts, for all is vanity.

Do not read that as the Bible teaching that you are an animal. Genesis has already told us that man was made in the image of God. The Preacher is speaking from under the sun, from the vantage point of what the eye can see. And what the eye sees at a graveside is that the king and the cow are equally still. Breath goes out. The word is ruach, which can mean breath, wind, or spirit, and the Preacher plays on that. All go to one place. All are from the dust, and to dust all return. That is a deliberate echo of God's words to Adam in Genesis three. The humbling is the point. The proud judges of verse sixteen are made of the same stuff as the people they cheat, and the same end waits for them.

Then comes verse twenty-one, the question that readers argue about. Who knows whether the spirit of man goes upward and the spirit of the beast goes down to the earth? Is the Preacher doubting that there is life after death? Careful commentators say no, and also not quite yes. The Cambridge Bible says of this question, quote, the words imply a strictly sceptical rather than a negative answer. End quote. In other words, he is not denying it. He is saying that from under the sun, by observation alone, nobody can demonstrate it. You cannot measure it from the outside. Which is exactly why the book needs more than observation. Just before this, he said that God has put eternity in the human heart. Now he admits we cannot prove from the outside where it leads. That is an honest pressure, and it leaves you leaning toward revelation, toward being told.

So what does he conclude? Verse twenty-two. So I saw that there is nothing better than that a man should rejoice in his work, for that is his lot. Who can bring him to see what will be after him? The word lot means portion, the share assigned to you. You will not fix every injustice, and you cannot see past the grave by squinting. So take the portion you have been given, work at it gladly, and leave the Judge his job. That is not escape. It is the freedom of knowing that you are not the one holding the scales. Think of how that lands on an ordinary Thursday. You may not be asked to fix a court. You may only be asked to do honest work, pay an honest wage, and tell the truth when it costs you. The Preacher says that is a portion worth rejoicing in. It is small, but it is yours, and God has handed it to you himself.

John one, verses forty-three through fifty-one.

The next day, Jesus decided to go to Galilee. John keeps counting days, and this is the fourth. Jesus finds Philip and says two words, follow me. Notice that this is the first time in John's Gospel that Jesus himself does the calling. Andrew and the other disciple went after him. Andrew went and got Simon. But Philip is found. John adds that Philip was from Bethsaida, the city of Andrew and Peter, a fishing town on the northeast shore of the Sea of Galilee. So there is a little cluster of friends from one town, and something is spreading among them.

And what does Philip do with it? He does what Andrew did. He finds Nathanael and says, we have found him of whom Moses in the Law and also the prophets wrote, Jesus of Nazareth, the son of Joseph. Look at how much he packs in. Moses and the prophets, which means the whole Old Testament. The Messiah everyone had been waiting for. And then the very ordinary address, Jesus of Nazareth, son of Joseph. Philip is excited and a little clumsy, and I like him for it.

Nathanael's answer is the sneer we began with. Can anything good come out of Nazareth? Nazareth was a small, obscure village, never named in the Old Testament. It was a nothing town. Some of that is local pride, since Nathanael was from Cana, a few miles away. But some of it is a real theological worry. Where in the Scriptures does the Messiah come from a place like that?

And look at what Philip does not do. He does not argue. He does not win the debate. He says, come and see. That is the same invitation Jesus gave yesterday, now in Philip's mouth. Charles Spurgeon preached on this passage in March of 1870, and he noticed how Nathanael responded. Quote. As soon as he was told to come and see he did come and see. End quote. That is the whole of it. Doubt did not stop him from walking over to look. The honest skeptic and the honest seeker can look very much alike, and Jesus knows which is which.

Then Jesus sees him coming. Behold, an Israelite indeed, in whom there is no deceit. The Greek word is dolos, which means a trick, a bait, a crooked angle. Now be careful, because Jesus is not saying Nathanael is sinless. The Pulpit Commentary is precise about this. Quote. Christ does not say that this man is sinless, but guileless. End quote. Nathanael says what he thinks. He said Nazareth was nothing, and he said it to a friend's face. There is no pretense in him.

There is also a quiet wordplay here. The first man to be called Israel was Jacob, whose name meant supplanter, the trickster who cheated his brother and deceived his blind father. Jesus calls Nathanael an Israelite with no deceit, no Jacob in him. The Cambridge Bible catches it. Quote. The supplanter is gone; the prince remains. End quote. Hold that, because Jacob is going to come back in verse fifty-one.

Nathanael is startled. How do you know me? And Jesus says, before Philip called you, when you were under the fig tree, I saw you. We are not told what Nathanael was doing there. The fig tree was a place of shade and quiet, and many have thought it was a place of prayer or reading Scripture. Spurgeon, in that same sermon, called him a man of secret prayer. Whatever it was, it was private, and nobody had seen it. And Spurgeon says what Jesus's words mean. Quote. Jesus saw thee; he knew thy desires, he read thy longings, he saw thee through and through. End quote. The unsettling and wonderful thing is that Nathanael came to see Jesus, and discovered that he had been seen first.

Nathanael breaks. Rabbi, you are the Son of God. You are the King of Israel. Now here is a question worth asking. Is that not a big leap from a fig tree? Does he truly understand what he is saying? Chrysostom, preaching on this chapter in the fourth century, noticed that other men in the Gospels will say the very same words and mean something different. Quote. Peter and Nathanael both spoke the same words, but not both with the same intention. End quote. His point is that the words alone are not the measure. What matters is what sits behind them. Nathanael says too much, perhaps, and Jesus gently does not correct him. He says, you will see greater things than these.

And then the solemn promise. Truly, truly, I say to you, you will see heaven opened, and the angels of God ascending and descending on the Son of Man. Here is Jacob again. Jacob, running from his brother, slept in the open at Bethel and dreamed of a ladder set on the earth with its top reaching to heaven, and angels going up and down on it. Jacob woke and said, this is the gate of heaven. Jesus says that the ladder is not a place. It is a person. The traffic between heaven and earth runs through the Son of Man. Nathanael, the man without Jacob's guile, is promised what Jacob saw.

The hardest question here is why Jesus says Son of Man rather than the title Nathanael has just used. Nathanael said Son of God and King of Israel, true and royal titles. Jesus answers with a title that includes humanity and suffering and glory together. It is the title he uses of himself in John more than any other, and it quietly says that the King of Israel will be the one on whom heaven opens and who also, in time, is lifted up.

So what do we do with this? Here is the application, and it is a small one. Philip did not have the answers to Nathanael's objection. He had a friend and a short invitation. When someone you love sneers at Jesus, your job is not to win the argument. It is to say, come and see, and then to get out of the way, because the one who saw Nathanael under the fig tree is very good at seeing.

One more thing about the fig tree, because it is easy to rush past. Think about how much of your life happens where no one is watching. The unanswered prayer in the car. The long night with a worry. The small private decision to forgive or not to forgive. The Preacher says that under the sun, nobody sees past the grave. Jesus says to Nathanael that he saw him before anyone called. Those two claims sit side by side in today's readings, and they do not cancel each other. The Preacher is telling you what your eyes can see. Jesus is telling you who sees you.

James two, verses fourteen through twenty-six.

Now the part of the Bible that many people find the hardest. What good is it, my brothers, if someone says he has faith but does not have works? Can that faith save him? Notice the way James frames the question. He does not ask whether works can save. He asks about someone who says he has faith. The claim is on the table, and James wants to know whether the claim is true.

His first picture is blunt. A brother or sister is poorly clothed and lacking daily food, and one of you says, go in peace, be warmed and filled, without giving them the things needed for the body. What good is that? You can hear that this follows directly from the first half of the chapter. Last time we saw a church that fawned over a rich man and shoved the poor man to a corner. Now a church that has kind words and empty hands. Faith by itself, if it does not have works, is dead.

Then James brings in a skeptical voice. Someone will say, you have faith and I have works. And James answers, show me your faith apart from your works, and I will show you my faith by my works. That is not a contest between two different ways of being saved. It is a challenge about evidence. Faith is invisible. How would anyone know?

Then comes verse nineteen, one of the sharpest lines in the Bible. You believe that God is one; you do well. Even the demons believe, and shudder. Think about that for a moment. The demons have correct theology. They know there is one God. They have no doubt. And what do they do? They shudder. Matthew Poole says that the word, quote, signifies extreme fear and horror. End quote. They believe, and it terrifies them, and it changes nothing about their rebellion.

John Gill, the Baptist, takes it to its conclusion. A faith like theirs, he says, is not nothing. It is real knowledge. But, quote, a man may have all faith of this kind, and be damned. End quote. That is a sobering sentence. Agreement with the right facts is not the same as trust. The Cambridge Bible puts the whole balance of James and Paul in one line. Quote. St Paul reproves the deadness of mere morality, St James that of mere orthodoxy. End quote. Paul is aiming at people who think good behavior earns God's favor. James is aiming at people who think correct belief is enough while their hands stay in their pockets.

Then James gives two examples, and they could not be more different. First, Abraham. Was not Abraham our father justified by works when he offered up his son Isaac on the altar? James then says, you see that faith was active along with his works, and faith was completed by his works. He then quotes Genesis fifteen, verse six, Abraham believed God, and it was counted to him as righteousness. Notice that. James agrees with Genesis. Abraham was counted righteous when he believed, decades before the altar. What happened on Mount Moriah in Genesis twenty-two was the believing made visible. The word completed means brought to its goal. Faith that is alive grows hands.

And the word justified does not always carry its Pauline meaning of declared righteous before God. In James's argument, it is the question of whether a man's claim to faith is shown to be true. Abraham is called the friend of God at the end of that story. The proof was in the walking.

Second, Rahab. Was not also Rahab the prostitute justified by works when she received the messengers and sent them out by another way? Think who she is. A woman in Jericho, a Canaanite, a prostitute, the opposite of Abraham in every respect. And yet she is James's second witness. Joshua two tells us she had heard what God did at the Red Sea and said, the Lord your God is he who is God in heaven above and on earth beneath. Then she hid the spies at the risk of her life. Her confession came first, and then her hiding of the messengers. Same pattern, different people. A father of the faith and a woman from the wrong side of every line. Faith found both, and it showed itself in both.

How do we reconcile this with Paul? The answer is the one the church has given since the Reformation, and a Tabletalk devotional from Ligonier states it in one sentence. Quote. James reminds us that though we are justified by faith alone, the faith that justifies us is never alone. End quote. The Westminster Confession says the same thing in its chapter on justification, that faith is the alone instrument of justification, yet it is never alone in the person justified, but is ever accompanied with all other saving graces. Paul and James are fighting different enemies. Paul fights people who would add works to faith as the ground. James fights people who would subtract works from faith as the fruit.

James ends with an image that will not leave you. For as the body apart from the spirit is dead, so also faith apart from works is dead. Matthew Poole presses the picture. Quote. A dead body, though called a body, is really but a carcass. End quote. It has the shape of a person and nothing else. It has the right doctrine in the right place and no breath.

And that word is spirit, the same word the Preacher was using this morning. Breath. Faith without works is a body without breath. Hold that thought, because it brings us to the close.

Closing.

Let me draw out one connection, and only because I think it is real. In Ecclesiastes, everything under the sun ends in the same dust, and the Preacher says, who knows? In John, Jesus answers part of that. He sees Nathanael under the fig tree, and heaven is opened over the Son of Man. And in James, we are told that a living faith, a breathing faith, is one that moves. Each reading is about what is seen and what is not. Nathanael was seen when he did not know it. The Preacher cannot see what comes after. James says faith has to be visible, in the open, where a hungry person can find it.

So here is your question for the day. When someone sneers, or when something looks like Nazareth to you, can you say come and see? And then, when you have seen, what will your hands do about it?

This has been Morning Mercies. Grace and peace.
